The Discovery Certification Protocol, or DCP, a new framework for auditing whether an AI research agent actually discovered something, as opposed to getting a high score that a different, simpler process could have reached. Lead authors Jingjie Ning, Shanshan Zhong, and Xiaochuan Li, from the School of Computer Science at Carnegie Mellon University, with Ji Zeng as the senior contributor. The motivation is a real and growing problem. AI research agents now routinely report that they found a new algorithm, a better program, or a useful construction, and they back that up with a benchmark score. But a score, standing alone, does not tell you whether the agent did the work. It might have stumbled onto the answer through luck, or through a route that was already publicly available, or because the benchmark was leaky. The protocol asks three specific, measurable questions about one reported result, and it does so with pre-registered rules so that the audit cannot be gamed after the fact.

The three questions are the whole design. Gate one asks whether the result is actually useful: does it beat a registered baseline by a minimum margin, measured on sealed evaluation data that was held back during the run? Gate two asks whether the outcome is recoverable without the agent's own research history. You give a fresh, matched agent the same starting information, the same tools, the same budget, and every web page the original agent saw, but you withhold the agent's internal run history, its hypotheses and trial results. If that fresh agent can still reach essentially the same score, then the discovery is not really a discovery; it was reachable from public and starting knowledge alone. Gate three, which is optional, asks whether the agent's own feedback loop did real work: it runs fresh paired experiments where one branch gets truthful feedback from its own actions and the other gets a neutral, non-directional feedback policy, and measures the difference.

The statistical machinery is deliberately conservative. In gate two, the challenger is a fresh episode drawn from a registered distribution, and the rule for a recovery is simply that the challenger produces a valid artifact whose score is at least the target score minus a small registered tolerance. If any recovery happens, the Core claim is immediately refuted, recorded as recovered. If no recovery happens across many episodes, you get a finite-sample upper bound on the probability that a recovery is possible at all. That bound has to fall below a registered threshold. So the Core decision requires not just zero observed recoveries, but a statistically defensible claim that recoveries are unlikely, plus adequate positive controls that prove the audit setup could have detected a recovery if one were available. That last part matters, because a zero-hit result is only meaningful if you know your controls would have fired.

They test the complete protocol on two real tasks. The first is a SQLite query-optimization problem: an agent must choose four partial indexes for a database with sixteen query families, four of which carry heavy hidden traffic. The agent, running on a DeepSeek V4 flash endpoint, improves the workload score from a baseline of zero to 0.8855, meaning roughly an 89 percent reduction in virtual machine work. In gate two, fresh challengers who got all the same starting information and the same two public documentation pages the agent read, but none of the agent's reasoning, reached at most 0.6734, well below the recovery line of 0.8805. Across 96 episodes there were zero recoveries, and the upper bound on recovery probability came out to 0.0468, below the threshold. The second task is a virtual catalyst optimization with five controls and eight levels each, 32,768 possible recipes, where the agent scores 1.0 against a baseline of 0.5990, and again zero recoveries in 96 episodes with the same 0.0468 bound.

The feedback result in gate three is the most interesting of the three. In both tasks, when the paired branches were run, the branch that got truthful feedback from its own experiments recovered the target 30 out of 30 times, while the neutral-feedback branch recovered zero out of 30. That is a clean, large effect, and it is what you would want to see: the agent's ability to read its own experimental results is doing real work, not just the starting knowledge. They also ran 60-pair null calibration studies to make sure the neutral channel itself was not accidentally informative, and those came out at a contrast of zero, inside the registered band. So the feedback effect is real and not an artifact of the neutral policy leaking information.

There is a calibration case that shows the protocol working in the other direction. A multidimensional knapsack task, where the target improved on a baseline and one matched challenger episode produced two valid solutions above the recovery line. That is exactly the case where the Core claim should fail, and it does: the protocol records it as recovered, with the Core decision refuted. The point of the protocol is not to certify everything; it is to have a decision rule that fires correctly in both directions, and the controlled cases are designed to exercise both. A low-sample affine case, meanwhile, exercises the audit-incomplete decision, where the positive controls do not fully resolve the adequacy requirement, so the protocol correctly refuses to make a clean call rather than forcing one.

It is worth pausing on what gate two actually rules out, because it is a subtle point that the paper is careful about. Withholding the run history is not the same as withholding the knowledge. The challenger gets the full registered background, the initial observations, the tools, and the complete set of web bytes the target agent saw. So the test is not whether the agent's private chain of thought was necessary; it is whether the outcome was reachable from the public and starting information at all, given the same budget and tools. A recovery means the agent's specific reasoning was not load-bearing for the result, even if the result is useful. That is a meaningful distinction, because a lot of agent-reported discoveries are plausibly of exactly that kind: genuinely useful, but reachable by any competent agent with the same access, so the credit belongs to the access and the benchmark, not to the run. The protocol does not call those bad; it just refuses to call them discoveries, which is the right epistemic stance for an auditor.

The trust model is another part of the design that deserves attention. The authors explicitly treat the claimant and the evidence producer as potentially strategic, which means the audit is built to catch a motivated reporter who might cherry-pick episodes, swap in a stronger model than registered, or peek at private scores early. The independent audit authority holds the sealed test and anchors the records before execution, and the complete ledgers expose omitted attempts and altered evidence. A deterministic, LLM-free verifier then recomputes the decisions from the frozen evidence bundles, so an independent reader can replay the whole audit without trusting the original producer's narrative. That separation between evidence production and decision checking is what makes the framework reusable across agent implementations, and it is the part most likely to survive even if the specific gates get revised. The honest limits are that the two complete audits are on relatively small, controlled tasks, one database optimization and one virtual experimental search, and the statistical power is bounded by the episode counts. But the conceptual contribution is substantial: a common, pre-registered evidence language that separates a useful result, a recoverable outcome, and a beneficial feedback policy into three distinct, independently testable claims, with a deterministic verifier that can replay the decision from frozen evidence. For a field that increasingly reports agent discoveries as benchmark scores, that separation is the missing piece.
