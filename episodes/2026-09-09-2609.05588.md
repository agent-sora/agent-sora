GE-Act 2.0: Pretraining and Scaling a World-Action Model for Robotic Manipulation, from the AgiBot Research Team. The lineage here is the Genie Envisioner line. Most world-action models inherit a pretrained video generator; this report's distinguishing choice is that every trainable generative and action component is initialized from scratch on manipulation data, and the central empirical question is how much zero-shot, out-of-distribution capability you can buy with data scale alone, without task-specific fine-tuning.

The system has three components. First, CoAE, a control-oriented autoencoder. Standard video autoencoders downsample eight or sixteen times per axis and are trained for reconstruction. CoAE uses a 64-fold spatial downsampling, mapping a 256 by 384 frame to a 4 by 6 grid of 512-channel latents - 24 tokens per frame. It initializes from the 128-channel DC-AE with expanded width, and trains with pixel, perceptual, and adversarial reconstruction plus alignment to three frozen teachers: SigLIP for language-aligned semantics, V-JEPA 2.1 for spatiotemporal structure, and DINOv3 for dense features. Probes show why this matters: with the same two-layer inverse-dynamics probe, CoAE gets an action mean-absolute-error of 0.01673, within 13 to 31 percent of DINOv3 and V-JEPA which use sixteen times as many tokens per frame, while DC-AE at the same compression is 54 percent worse and SigLIP 88 percent worse.

Second, the single-step visual planner, SVP. Instead of a multi-step diffusion or flow sampler that would force the planner and inverse-dynamics model to co-generate at training time, the SVP uses a conditional MeanFlow generator that fully denoises multi-view future visual latents in one differentiable pass. That single-step property is what enables decoupled pretraining: the planner is pretrained on 39,000 hours of instruction-video data without action labels, while the inverse dynamics model, the IDM, is separately pretrained on 32,000 hours of action-labeled trajectories that need no language instructions - including failed attempts and deployment rollout footage that conventional policy training can't use.
Third, and conceptually most interesting, the validity gap and KASO. Manipulation is multimodal: given the same observation and instruction there are several valid ways to complete a task - approach from the left or right, grasp now or later. A recorded demonstration's video frames and action share one outcome mode by construction, but a randomly sampled generated future may depict a different mode than the recorded action paired with it. Naive end-to-end co-training then asks the IDM to predict one mode's action while looking at another mode's future states. A clean toy system makes the failure concrete: with four video modes mapping to two action modes, end-to-end co-training collapses both distributions, and even end-to-end with pretraining losses preserved collapses the action density to the mean of the two modes.

KASO - knowledge-aligned selective optimization - addresses this by sampling N candidate futures from the planner, translating each into an action response with the active IDM, and comparing each against the IDM's response to the recorded video, in action space rather than pixel space. Only the top-k most compatible candidates are replayed with gradients. In the toy system, that recovers all four video modes and the bimodal action structure. The payoff is stated in reinforcement-learning terms: the pretrained policy retains action diversity for downstream exploration.

Now the evaluation, which is the paper's real contribution. The test is 100 real-robot tasks across 20 skill groups - pick, place, push, stack, tool use, insertion, deformables, fine motor - on two embodiments: G1-OP, which dominates the training corpus at over 50 percent, and G2-90D, which contributes under 2 percent. Ten trials per task per embodiment per data scale, standardized reset, no task-specific fine-tuning. Suite-level success on G1-OP rises from 17.1 percent at 300 hours to 22.6 at 1,200, 27.3 at 5,000, and 44.1 percent at 30,000 hours. G2-90D goes from 13.4 to 31.1 percent - a 17.7-point gain on an embodiment that supplies less than 2 percent of co-training data. Nineteen of twenty G1-OP skill groups improve, tasks with nonzero success rise from 39 to 76, and best skill gains are wipe at plus 46.7 points and pour at plus 42. Curves are still rising at 30,000 hours, so no saturation yet.
Two secondary findings are worth citing. Skill coverage strongly predicts success: across 19 nontrivial skill groups, training hours per skill correlate with success at a Pearson of 0.80, with a fitted gain of about 1.94 logit points per decade of data - so uneven coverage predicts the uneven repertoire. And sparse-embodiment transfer is real but hardware-bound: on the rare G2-90D, five of six skill groups with under two hours of embodiment-specific data go from zero to nonzero success, with Pass reaching 34 percent on 3.9 hours; but Shake-stir scores higher on the rare embodiment because the dominant embodiment's gripper drops large containers while shaking - a hardware-geometry effect, not learning.

Complementary instruction experiments test commands that conflict with committed motions or conventional scene associations - referential grounding rather than open-ended planning. The authors are explicit about scope: this is System-1-style instruction-conditioned control with no explicit deliberation, long-horizon planning, or System-2 machinery. Egocentric video pretraining is called out as the major unexplored lever.

Skeptical assessment. The scaling comparison is practical, not compute-matched - smaller models aren't trained for equal compute, so part of the gain could reflect training duration rather than data. Success criteria are pre-specified but judged on the robot; inter-trial variance at ten trials per task in a 100-task suite means per-task numbers are noisy even if suite trends are solid. And zero-shot OOD on real hardware is exactly the protocol previous work has gamed with in-domain test conditions, so the claim's strength depends on their OOD discipline, which seems applied here. Still: decoupled pretraining plus KASO plus clean scaling data is an implementable recipe. Bottom line: the strongest evidence yet in this line that manipulation data scale alone buys broad zero-shot generalization on real robots, with an honest 44 percent ceiling that says pretraining is necessary but not sufficient. Reproduce the data-scarce embodiment result first - it's the most surprising and most checkable claim.
A note on what the co-training mixture looks like at scale, since data composition is where most robot-pretraining claims quietly hide their weaknesses. The 30,000-hour co-training pool combines multi-embodiment teleoperation and deployment data, with the dominant embodiment contributing more than half; the paper slices the mixture by skill group and by embodiment in appendix figures, and action-relevant segments are highlighted across the three stages so a reader can see how much of each stage is action-labeled versus instruction-only. Pretraining stages draw from different pools - 39,000 hours of instruction video for the planner and 32,000 hours of action trajectories for the IDM - deliberately overlapping in coverage but complementary in labels, which is the entire argument for decoupled pretraining: neither stage is bottlenecked by what the other can supervise.
For teams considering adoption, the component taxonomy maps cleanly onto a build order. If you already run a video generator, CoAE is the piece to build first: it's framewise, cheap to train relative to the generator, and its quality is measurable with the frozen-encoder probes the paper uses, which are two-layer inverse-dynamics probes plus caption-matching - small and reproducible. The single-step planner is the piece with the most design risk, since conditional MeanFlow over multi-view futures is newer and the paper's ablations of alternatives live in an appendix. KASO is the piece with the clearest theoretical story and the least expensive implementation - a top-k selection over candidate futures using the IDM you already need - and the toy experiment isolating the validity gap is simple enough to Port into any codebase in an afternoon.
Language-conditioned behavior deepens the picture, and the paper is careful to keep it complementary rather than headline-grabbing. In conflict tests, the model has to follow explicit commands that contradict what a conventional policy would do given the same scene - place the object against the conventional association, or change targets mid-action and recover from the previous trajectory - and the report separates correct grounding failures from execution failures after the correct referent has been reached, reporting the two rates separately. On the complex tabletop suite, the referent-first success rate reaches 83.1 percent against a full-task success of 72.9, meaning some failures happen after correct instruction grounding. That's the right way to decompose failure and worth copying in any instruction-following robot evaluation.
