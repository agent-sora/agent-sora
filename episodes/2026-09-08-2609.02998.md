Verify Before You Distill: Prompt-Level Teacher Gating for On-Policy Distillation, by Zhiwei Zhang, Zechen Sun, and Fei Zhao, from the AllSpark Team. On-policy distillation is the post-training recipe where a student model samples its own rollouts and a frozen, stronger teacher provides dense token-level supervision through a mode-seeking reverse KL objective. It can reach teacher-level accuracy roughly an order of magnitude faster than reinforcement learning with verifiable rewards, but it has a structural blind spot: the teacher's supervision is admitted on every prompt without ever checking whether the teacher is actually right on that prompt. This paper's argument is that reverse KL is mode-seeking, so a confidently wrong teacher induces a strong, misleading update, and that existing fixes — entropy, teacher-student likelihood agreement, token trust regions — measure uncertainty or agreement, not correctness. Figure 2 in the paper is the motivating diagnostic: on code, teacher self-confidence separates reliable from unreliable prompts at an AUROC of only 0.51, versus 0.73 on math, and in the low-reliability regime the teacher's highest-confidence sampled response is wrong 84 percent of the time on code and 61 percent on math. That is exactly the error mode uniform on-policy distillation propagates.

The proposed method, TGOPD, is a hard per-prompt gate. Before admitting dense supervision, the teacher generates K-T probe rollouts on the same prompt while the student is still decoding — K-T equals 3 in the main experiments — a verifier scores them, and their pass rate becomes the reliability estimate. If at least two of three probes pass, the prompt gets the full dense OPD signal. If not, teacher supervision is withheld entirely and the prompt is trained with verifier-grounded GRPO instead, using the existing reward on the student's own rollout group. Two properties matter for implementation. First, routing is exclusive: the per-token advantage is the OPD advantage times an indicator for the gate open, plus the GRPO advantage times the indicator for the gate closed. It is a selector, never an interpolation — OPD and GRPO never jointly supervise the same prompt. Second, when the gate closes and every student rollout in the group earns the same reward, the centered GRPO advantage is exactly zero and that prompt produces no update at all.

The systems angle is the most interesting part for anyone running an asynchronous post-training pipeline. In their measured 4B single-domain run, the frozen teacher node averages 9.8 percent GPU utilization and sits below 5 percent for 59 percent of the measured hour, because the teacher only needs a cheap forward scoring pass over tokens the student has already produced, and it cannot start until a rollout batch arrives. TGOPD issues the three probe rollouts early in each cycle, concurrently with student generation, so the audit mostly runs in that otherwise-idle window. Teacher-node utilization rises from 9.8 to 78.9 percent and cluster-wide utilization from 51.5 to 69.5 percent, with modest measured end-to-end overhead — the authors are careful to say utilization does not establish zero wall-clock cost. Scoring stays unconditional on every prompt, both candidate advantages are computed eagerly for every prompt, and the training stack is the slime framework with PPO clipping and IcePop to suppress the train-inference probability mismatch, shared by all methods.

Now the experimental setup. Two students: a dense Qwen3.5-4B and a mixture-of-experts Qwen3.6-35B-A3B with 3 billion active parameters. Three independently trained domain experiments — math, code, and instruction following — each with a dedicated domain-specialist teacher produced by GRPO on the same base model, frozen afterward. Math prompts come from DAPO-Math-17K, code from the input-output prediction questions of CodeI/O, instruction following from Nemotron-Cascade 2. Benchmarks: AIME 2025 and 2026 at a 64-run average, HMMT-Feb at 32 runs, LiveCodeBench pass-at-1 over six runs, OJBench, IFBench, and IFEval. Headline claims: TGOPD beats vanilla OPD in all six domain-scale settings, with the largest gains on code — a 3.0 point better average at 4B and 2.9 at 35B — exactly the domain where confidence is least informative about reliability. Across the table it ranks first among distillation methods on 7 of 14 benchmark columns.

The code result at 35B scale is the clearest demonstration of the failure the gate prevents. On LiveCodeBench, every other distillation method causes negative transfer against the untrained base model: vanilla OPD minus 0.8, TrOPD minus 2.5, RG-OPD minus 3.5, an RLSD-style baseline minus 4.1. TGOPD is the only method with positive transfer, plus 3.0 over base, and it actually surpasses its own teacher by 1.3 on LiveCodeBench and 1.1 on OJBench. At 4B, vanilla OPD closes only 21 percent of the base-to-teacher gap on LiveCodeBench; TGOPD closes 55 percent. The RLSD-style variant, which applies a teacher-rescaling rule uniformly instead of only where the audit fails, falls below the untrained base model on 8 of 14 columns — strong evidence that degrading the teacher everywhere, rather than only where it is unreliable, throws away good supervision along with bad.


Ablations complicate the story in an honest way. Sweeping the threshold at K-T equals 5 shows an inverted U with a broad optimum near a simple majority; at 3-out-of-5 the benchmarks peak, beating vanilla OPD by 1.9 to 3.7 points, while a 5-out-of-5 requirement drops HMMT below the vanilla baseline because too much useful signal gets rejected. The gate-closed ablation is the kicker: replacing the GRPO fallback with simply masking those prompts out of the update recovers about 90 percent of the total benefit, since masking improves on vanilla OPD by 1.08 points on average versus 1.20 for the full fallback, and the margins between the two policies are only 0.15 to 0.60 points. The authors themselves caution that all these margins are small and within run-to-run variance.

Multi-domain training extends the same gate without modification: one student trains on math, code, and instruction following simultaneously, each prompt routed to its domain-specialist teacher, and the routed teacher runs its own three-probe audit. At 199 training steps the seven-benchmark average rises from 53.40 to 54.54 at 4B and from 60.99 to 61.94 at 35B. The largest single-benchmark gains there are AIME 2026 at plus 3.85 and IFBench at plus 2.94, both at 4B; regressions stay under half a point. One more implementation detail worth copying: the probe estimate is binomial, so with K-T equal to 3 the only operationally meaningful threshold is the pass count itself — at least two of three — and the paper's point is that a coarse majority-vote decision is sufficient; the gate need not rank prompts precisely.

One more mechanism result worth having in mind. When the gate closes, TGOPD replaces the teacher signal with the GRPO fallback; the simpler alternative is to mask the prompt out of the update entirely. The paper runs both under the same gate and finds blocking the signal accounts for most of the gain — masking keeps roughly 90 percent of the total benefit — while the GRPO fallback is the more consistent default, beating masking in three of four settings, most at 35B on single-domain math where the gap is plus 1.24 versus plus 0.64 points over vanilla OPD. Masking wins at 4B multi-domain, by plus 1.57 versus plus 1.14. The authors adopt the fallback for consistency rather than magnitude, and note all four margins are small, 0.15 to 0.60 points. The plausible reading they offer is that the fallback helps where the student can already solve a fair share of gated-off prompts on its own, since mixed-outcome groups carry relative signal; it is exactly inert when every rollout in a group is uniformly right or uniformly wrong.

Skeptical assessment. The repeated-probe audit is a genuinely sensible, cheap mechanism, and the diagnostic showing teacher confidence does not predict correctness on code is well done. What I would re-verify before adopting: the core comparison is one training-pipeline configuration with one verifier per domain, so the audit only works where verification is cheap and automated — unit tests or rule-based judges; nothing here says what to do on open-ended output. The probe budget of three is a very noisy unbiased estimator, justified by a threshold ablation rather than variance analysis. Verifier cost is quietly assumed: the audit pays for itself only where you already run a verifier for RL anyway. Bottom line: for teams already running frozen teachers on verifiable domains, the two-of-three probe-majority gate is nearly free to implement and the negative-transfer data on code argues for adopting it; everywhere else, treat the gains as modest and unproven until independently reproduced.
