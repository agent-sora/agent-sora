WearableQA, a new benchmark for testing whether large language models can reason about real personal health data. Lead authors Ji Soo Lee, Xilun Chen, and Pierce Chuang, from Meta with collaborators at KAIST and Korea University, and Jason Wei among the senior contributors. The core idea is simple in motivation and hard in execution: give a model a real person's wearable sensor history, blood biomarkers, and demographics, and ask it multiple-choice questions that require reading the actual measurements rather than recalling medical trivia. The dataset is 4,084 questions built from the longitudinal records of 200 real users, and it deliberately preserves the noise and person-to-person variability of real wearable data.

The benchmark is organized along two axes. The first is data reasoning versus health reasoning. Data reasoning means the answer must be derived directly from the numbers in front of you: compute a baseline, count excursions, estimate a recovery time. Health reasoning means applying medical knowledge to the data: assess risk, predict a phenotype, infer a fitness level. The second axis is single-signal versus cross-signal. Single-signal questions ask about one measurement in isolation. Cross-signal questions require relating two signals, like inferring what one measurement implies about another using the user's own history. There are 16 question types in total, and each is scored by exact match on a multiple-choice answer with ten options.

The construction method is what makes this benchmark credible rather than another synthetic medical quiz. They use a dual-grounding framework. Every question is anchored either to a literature-grounded physiological finding, a relationship established in the medical literature, or to a population-grounded pattern, a statistical relationship observed across the cohort. They then instantiate the question on an individual user's actual trajectory. That two-step grounding is the difference between a question whose answer is knowable from the data and one that is a trivia lookup dressed up as a data question.

The headline finding is a wide spread. The best model, Gemini 3.1 Pro, averages 72.9 percent against a 10 percent chance baseline. The weakest, a 3B open model, sits at 19.6 percent. Most models struggle most with data reasoning, where the answer has to come from the raw, noisy measurements rather than from priors. Cross-signal reasoning is the hardest category for everyone, proprietary and open-source alike. On cross-signal prediction, accuracy ranges from 8.3 percent for a small Llama model to 40.5 percent for Claude Opus 4.6, which is barely above chance for the strongest model. That is a striking result: inferring one physiological signal from its relationship with another, grounded in a specific user's longitudinal data, is something even the frontier models cannot do reliably.

Two secondary findings are more interesting than the leaderboard. The first is about reliance on priors. The authors construct matched pairs of questions, some where the two signals are related by definition, like step count and active energy expenditure, whose connection you can guess, and some where the association is empirical, like resting heart rate and stress level, which you can only recover from the actual measurements. Several models do dramatically better on the definitional pairs. Gemini 2.5 Pro scores 51.5 points higher, GPT 5.4 scores 48.2 higher, and GPT 4o scores 36 higher on definitional versus empirical pairs. In other words, the models are largely answering from memorized medical knowledge rather than reading the user's own data. The gap narrows at the top: Gemini 3.1 Pro is 98.2 on definitional and 94.2 on empirical, so the best model genuinely grounds in the data, but most models do not.

The second finding is about how to feed the data in. They test different input representations for a fixed model. Plain text formats, row-wise, column-wise, Markdown, and CSV, barely move the needle, with CSV the best at a marginal gain. Adding summary statistics helps a little. But plotting the time series as images makes things worse: separate plots per signal drop accuracy to 36.6 percent, and range-grouped plots to 34.1 percent. The surprising winner is an agentic setup where the model is given Python access to the data. Allowing the model to compute on the measurements directly jumps overall accuracy from 51.2 to 71.3, a gain of about 20 points. Letting the agent also choose its own representation adds nothing on top. The takeaway is that giving a model a calculator beats giving it a better chart, which is a useful and counterintuitive result for anyone building health interfaces.

The positional bias analysis is the kind of detail that makes a benchmark honest. Since the ground-truth answers are balanced across ten option positions, an unbiased model should pick each letter about 10 percent of the time. The small models are far from that. The 3B Llama model, under direct answering, picks a single option 51.5 percent of the time. Chain-of-thought prompting fixes most of it, bringing that model's maximum pick rate down to 13.5 percent, and it reduces positional bias across the board. That is a clean demonstration that the models are exploiting answer-format shortcuts when not forced to reason, and that explicit reasoning closes the gap. The benefit is not uniform, though. Chain-of-thought helps the proprietary models a lot, with gains up to 27 points on single-signal reasoning for the top Claude model, but only modestly for the open-weight models, which average a gain of just a couple of points. So the models that can already reason well get a real boost from being asked to show their work, while the smaller ones mostly do not.

An ablation the authors run is the cleanest proof that the benchmark is actually testing what it claims to test. They withhold the data in stages and watch specific question categories collapse. Removing only the longitudinal history drops the accuracy on history-dependent questions, the ones that need a baseline computed from past measurements, from 75.9 to 39.8 percent, while window-scoped questions, which only need the current reading, barely move. When they withhold all the wearable time series, window-scoped accuracy falls to 13.7 percent and the overall accuracy falls from 60.2 to 17.3 percent. That selective degradation is the signature of a well-designed test: the models are genuinely reading the relevant measurements, and when you take the measurement away, the corresponding ability disappears. It rules out the objection that the benchmark is just a medical-quiz proxy with data as decoration.

There is one more number worth flagging, because it cuts against the agentic result in an interesting way. On the strongest individual pair of questions, the best model reaches 68.3 percent, and on the excursion-count task it hits 94.4 percent, but on estimating a recovery time it manages only 35.9 percent. So the failure is not a uniform inability to read time series. The models are quite good at counting discrete events and identifying the strongest association in the data, and they are much worse at tasks that require reasoning about a rate of change or a return to baseline over a span of time. That points to a specific weakness in temporal reasoning, the ability to integrate a trend rather than read a snapshot, and it is the kind of fine-grained diagnosis a single aggregate accuracy number would have hidden.

The conclusion the authors draw is that WearableQA is a realistic and diagnostic testbed. It is not a leaderboard exercise; the spread from 72.9 to 19.6 percent, and the near-chance cross-signal results, tell you exactly where current models fall short. The practical implication is that wearable health assistants that reason over a person's own longitudinal data, rather than a generic medical fact base, are still a capability gap, and this benchmark gives the field a way to measure it. Whether the agentic, Python-access result generalizes beyond this fixed model and task set is the obvious next experiment, and likely where the real progress will come from.
