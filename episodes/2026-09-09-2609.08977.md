The Omni Interaction Agent Technical Report, presenting Gander. Lead contributors from the Tencent Hunyuan Speech Team, with Zhejiang University, Shanghai Jiao Tong University, The Chinese University of Hong Kong, and Nanyang Technological University. Gander is an end-to-end model that tries to unify three things in one system: continuous multimodal perception across video, speech, and text; real-time full-duplex interaction where users can interrupt at any time; and agentic task execution.

The report's first argument is architectural rather than empirical: true interactivity cannot be layered on top of a turn-based model with a voice activity detector. Pipelines built on VAD plus ASR handle request-response fine but struggle with hesitation, pauses, barge-in interruptions, overlapping speech, backchannels, and noisy multi-party scenes. Their answer is to make interaction state part of the model's own prediction - the model explicitly decides whether to listen or speak at each step of a streaming sequence.

Gander's central design is a Brain-Cerebellum split. The cerebellum is a streaming real-time interaction model running a Thinker-Talker architecture: streaming video and audio encoders feed a language backbone that operates over flattened one-second chunks, and a fast speech decoder renders audio. The Brain is a separate, heavier reasoning module - deliberately training-free and plug-and-play - that the cerebellum invokes through tool calls for long-horizon work like coding or file operations; the Brain's results come back asynchronously and the cerebellum folds them into the ongoing conversation. Because the Brain is not trained, better reasoning models can be swapped in without touching the interaction model.
Details on the front cerebellum, since that's the novel machine. Perception runs incrementally: video frames use any-resolution partitioning, each slice encoded by a SigLIP vision encoder and condensed by a query-based resampler into a small fixed set of tokens per slice - roughly a sixteen-fold reduction over the raw patch grid, more aggressive than the four-fold compression common in prior omni models, at resolution capped at 448 by 448. Audio goes through a chunk-wise speech encoder at about 50 frames per second, downsampled five times by a simple MLP projector to roughly 10 tokens per second.

The interaction format is one chunk per second containing three flattened segments: the encoded audio and visual tokens for that window, a control token, and however many text tokens the model emits - possibly zero. The control token takes one of three values: listen, meaning stay silent and keep observing; speak, which commits to producing text that the downstream speech decoder turns into audio; or interrupt, letting the model cut off its own in-progress utterance when the scene changes or the user starts talking. Context is a fixed sliding budget of 128 chunks - about two minutes - with the oldest chunks evicted as new ones arrive, keeping per-step cost flat over arbitrarily long sessions. Speech generation keeps the backbone in the text domain and offloads acoustics to a small speech-token decoder plus a streaming flow-matching waveform decoder conditioned on reference audio, which also gives zero-shot voice control.

The training corpus totals about 2.7 million items across four families. Speech interaction is roughly 37 percent; there's audio-visual interaction data; agentic interaction including 320 thousand audio agentic examples at nearly twelve percent; and robustness data, including 40 thousand no-command-environment examples teaching the model to stay silent under non-directed input, plus anti-interference and multi-party tracks. The duplex data construction is meticulous: competitive interruptions carry a hidden assistant continuation that temporally overlaps the user's incoming speech but is never heard, and backchannels are accepted only if embedded inside the interlocutor's ongoing utterance and under a length threshold of eight characters in Chinese or six words in English, with interrogative or requestive candidates demoted to ordinary turns. Backchannel phrasings come from a bilingual lexicon of 266 Chinese and 170 English expressions across eleven intent categories.
On the Brain side, an orchestration runtime has lean and coordinated modes: lean mode passes the user turn straight to the back brain for lower latency and more determinism, while the coordinator mode adds supervised decisions at the cost of extra invocations and nondeterminism. The back brain, per the paper, is a training-free general-purpose agent - citing Codex as the instantiation - with runtime interfaces to fetch context and send updates. That's the biggest compromise in the design and they say so: a trainable back brain receiving the full multimodal stream in real time is an open question.

Now the honest part: performance evaluation is thin. The authors state plainly that no dedicated benchmark exists for omni interaction agents, so they follow GPT-4o practice by reporting results on established benchmarks like BigBenchAudio and, otherwise, subjective demos on the project page. There is no quantitative full-duplex table, no interruption-latency numbers, no ablations of the chunk duration or the control-token design. For a technical report from a major lab effort, that's thin evidence for the central claims, and the two research questions they open with - is composable capability enough, and can one model be both fast and smart - are answered by design argument and demo rather than by measurement.

Skeptical read: the chunk-flattening full-duplex formulation is a sensible and complete design, and the data pipeline for interruptions and backchannels is the most reproducible artifact here. The Brain-Cerebellum split mirrors what OpenAI's GPT-Live and Claude's voice mode do with item queues, so the differentiator is doing it end-to-end and open-sourcing it. Bottom line: a credible blueprint for full-duplex multimodal agents with unusually careful duplex data construction, but with almost no quantified evidence yet - treat the capability claims as an architecture proposal to be verified, not results.
The Brain-side runtime deserves a closer look because it's the piece most teams will borrow first. The gateway maintains persistent entities: task records, execution state, and the delivery record for returning results to the user, which is how a task can survive across minutes of casual chatting. Task lifecycle is explicit - a task begins with a task-start tool call, continues with user modifications through task-send, and delivers results as declarative summaries back through the cerebellum, so the conversation stays the interface while the state lives elsewhere. The paper walks through open design questions in unusual detail: whether the cerebellum should add reasoning before delegating, whether to include voice transcription plus final keyframes or a richer frame set, and who summarizes results. Each has a lean answer and a coordinator answer, with the trade between determinism and adaptiveness made explicit - a genuinely useful engineering taxonomy even outside speech.
Where does this sit against the field? Against speech-only duplex models like Moshi and GPT-Live, Gander adds vision to the interaction state, which is what lets it resolve references like "that warning on slide 4" and do proactive reminders keyed to screen content. Against omni models like MiniCPM-o 4.5 and SeedRealtime, it adds the persistent agentic runtime rather than one-shot tool calling. The lineage claims are carefully given: chunk flattening follows interaction-model precedents, and the thinker-talker design follows Qwen Omni and MiniCPM with a Codex-style execution backend. So novelty is in the integration and in the duplex-plus-agent data recipe, not in any single component - and the paper is candid about that, positioning itself as a system and data contribution rather than an architectural one.
One underexplained engineering choice: the visual budget. Capping at 448 by 448 with 16-fold token compression per frame is defensible for latency, but for agentic work like reading a browser page or an IDE error text, that resolution is marginal, and the GUI-based omni agentic slice of the corpus is only 36 thousand items - about 1.3 percent of the corpus. The report shows the system attempting to read on-screen content and fix errors, but with this token budget the honest expectation is that fine-grained screen understanding is delegated to the back brain, not the cerebellum. That matches the architecture, but nobody should expect the front model to do high-resolution detail work.
