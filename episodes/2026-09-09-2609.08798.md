Eliciting Weak-to-Strong Generalization with On-Policy Reverse Distillation. First authors Youngrok Park and Sangmin Bae of KAIST AI, with Hojung Jung, and collaborators at Microsoft, the University of Toronto, and Mila at the Université de Montréal; Aaron Courville is a senior author. The question is weak-to-strong generalization: can a stronger student learn from a weaker teacher and end up surpassing it?

Conventional distillation treats the weak teacher as an optimization target, which risks imposing the teacher's capacity ceiling on the student. On-policy distillation helps with the distribution mismatch but still matches the teacher's policy directly. This paper's method, OPRD - On-Policy Reverse Distillation - takes a different route: it never makes the teacher's policy a target. Instead it computes the direction of the teacher's policy shift, learned during weak-model post-training, and uses that direction only to rescale the student's own verifier-driven policy gradient.

Mechanically: the teacher's policy shift relative to its reference policy gives a unit direction at each token. The student's policy gradient is decomposed into a component along that direction and an orthogonal remainder. The used update is the original gradient plus a nonnegative scale lambda times the projected component - so the projected part is amplified by one plus lambda and the orthogonal part passes through untouched. Because this map is invertible for nonnegative lambda, two properties hold: stationarity is preserved, the transformed gradient is zero exactly when the original is zero, and the inner product of original and transformed gradients is at least the original gradient's squared norm, with the extra term being lambda times the squared alignment coefficient. So greater alignment between student gradient and teacher direction buys greater first-order progress without adding a teacher-defined target.

Contrast this with the two failure modes the method is designed against. Plain verifier-only RL, GRPO, re-learns the task from scratch at the larger scale - slow, and it leaves the teacher's investment unused. Plain on-policy distillation, OPD, matches the frozen teacher token by token on student rollouts; the student inherits the teacher's ceiling partly because supervision is dense but teacher-defined. KDRL, the hybrid baseline, mixes verifier gradients with teacher matching and still underperforms because matching the full policy remains part of the objective. OPRD's design choice is to keep the verifier objective untouched - same objective, same stationary points - and to use the teacher only as a lightweight, per-token steering signal.

Data construction for the reasoning side is small but deliberate: 20,000 fixed examples per task, 19,800 train and 200 held out. Everything is verifier-scored; no human preferences. This keeps the study clean but means the conclusions inherit the cleanliness of the verifiers, which is exactly where a production deployment would mostly diverge from this setup.
One wrinkle worth implementing carefully: the sign of the alignment coefficient says whether the student gradient follows or opposes the teacher direction. Amplifying only one sign can systematically magnify reward-irrelevant bias, so OPRD uses asymmetric scaling: the positive-alignment branch is active immediately with strength lambda, while the negative branch - which supports verifier-backed departures from the teacher - ramps up gradually over a warm-up horizon. An ablation on the coefficient shows every positive value of lambda improves final pass rate over plain GRPO, gains speed up to lambda of 0.5, and performance changes little beyond that, so the method isn't knife-edge.

Setup: teachers are intermediate GRPO checkpoints of 4B-scale Qwen3 models; students are Qwen3 8B. Math training uses DAPO-Math-17K with evaluation on AIME 2024, AIME 2025, HMMT 2025, and OlympiadBench, reporting Mean at 16 samples. Reasoning tasks use four Reasoning Gym benchmarks - Knights and Knaves, Quantum Lock, String Manipulation, and Countdown - each with a fixed pool of 20,000 examples, reporting pass at 1. Results are averaged over checkpoints at steps 30 through 150 to summarize the whole learning curve.

The headline table: the base 8B student starts at a math average of 25.98 and a reasoning average of 5.04. GRPO gets to 39.38 and 41.81. On-policy distillation barely moves reasoning. But OPRD reaches 51.91 on math and 55.18 on reasoning - gains of 7.92 and 10.80 points over the strongest baseline - and notably the student surpasses its weak teacher, which scored 38.66 and 44.65. On the tighter comparison against other weak-to-strong methods, OPRD averages 60.81 across three tasks versus 54.21 for the best prior method, and one privileged-context baseline actually collapses to 21.69.
The multi-teacher setting is the one I'd watch for production consolidation. Four separately post-trained Qwen3-4B-Base task specialists - one each for the four Reasoning Gym tasks - are distilled into a single Qwen3-8B-Base student over 300 updates. Mix-RL averages 47.68, multi-teacher on-policy distillation plateaus at 43.10, and OPRD reaches 58.77, beating Mix-RL by 11.09 points and the specialist average by 14.12 - and it surpasses each individual specialist on all four tasks, so it's joint improvement rather than cross-task trading. The authors connect this to PCGrad-style projection, except the projection is between each task's student gradient and teacher direction rather than between conflicting task gradients. A plausible story, not proven: interference reduction is inferred, not directly measured.

Strong-to-weak results confirm the method doesn't depend on capacity ordering: distilling a Qwen3-8B teacher into a 1.7B student lifts AIME 2024 to 33.58, and on Knights and Knaves the base-to-base pairing scores 49.40, beating even teacher-level matching approaches by wide margins. Response-style analysis supports the intended reading: OPRD students stay closer to verifier-only RL models than to their weak teachers, meaning teacher guidance accelerated the student's own optimization rather than redirecting it.

Skeptical assessment. The teacher-direction construction requires storing the weak teacher's reference policy and computing its per-token shift during student training - that's a live teacher in the loop, and the report's strongest results depend on checkpoint teachers whose quality matters; the checkpoint-quality ablation shows results track teacher GRPO stage. The claim of surpassing the teacher relies on verifier rewards being clean; on noisy or hackable verifiers, amplified verifier-supported updates inherit verifier pathologies. And evaluation averages over early checkpoints, which flatters fast methods. Bottom line: a clean, well-motivated technique for getting weak-to-strong transfer that actually beats the teacher in the strongest setting, with multi-teacher consolidation as the immediate practical payoff. Verify the reward models before scaling it.
Why does extraction of a direction, rather than the shift itself, matter so much in practice? Because the weak teacher's post-training shift is learned inside the teacher's policy class. Its magnitude may reflect the small model's logic and may be useless, or actively harmful, for the 8B student's representational habits. Taking only the unit direction discards the magnitude and lets the student's own verifier-driven gradient set step size. The paper's framing is that the teacher contributes "which directions of change were worth learning," while the student's reward model decides "how far to go in them." That maps onto real consolidation pipelines where successive generations want to hand over capability summaries rather than raw logits, and it's a more transfer-friendly contract than full logit matching.
For an engineering reader, the strongest signal in the results section is how flat and consistent OPRD's gains are across benchmarks and across the two very different transfer directions. On Knights and Knaves, the student jumps from a base 5 percent pass rate to 73.3 - the largest absolute jump anywhere in the study - and on Countdown and String Manipulation, tasks where ordinary distillation and even GRPO barely move, OPRD still trends up steadily rather than plateauing like MOPD. Reading the tables row by row, the pattern "OPD matches OPRD early, then OPRD keeps improving past OPD's plateau" repeats in every setting, which is the empirical signature of the design: teacher guidance is genuinely useful while the student is weaker, and the divergence once the student has caught up is exactly what the reverse objective predicts.
Worth knowing before adopting: the method needs a student policy-gradient baseline plus a teacher policy-delta direction, which means storing the reference policy and the teacher throughout training - memory overhead scales with the larger student size, not the weak teacher. The rollout machinery is identical to GRPO's, so the added engineering is the per-token projection and the warm-up scheduler, both cheap. Anyone who has run on-policy distillation at scale knows the pain of teacher-student template mismatch; OPRD sidesteps none of that, and the paper doesn't claim to - the setup section handles same-family Qwen3 models only, so cross-family transfer - a 1.5B teacher distilling into a Llama-based student, say - remains untested.
