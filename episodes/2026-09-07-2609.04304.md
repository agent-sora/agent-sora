Iris: Climbing to the Search Frontier, by Ziyuan Liu, Hengqi Liu, and Zichuan Wang, from the AllSpark team. This is a search-agent report, not a new foundation model, but it is the top paper on today's feed by a wide margin, and it matters for anyone building agents that have to work from incomplete information. The team presents Iris-mini and Iris-pro, two search agents trained at 35 billion and 397 billion total parameters, both mixture-of-experts with 256,000 token context windows, initialized from Qwen3.6-35B-A3B and Qwen3.5-397B-A17B respectively. The headline claim is an end-to-end recipe: data construction, training, and evaluation for web search agents, with results reported under a controlled protocol that separates the policy from the inference harness.

The problem setup is that search agents have to do more than reason. They have to decide what to search for, how to interpret retrieved evidence, when to keep exploring, and when the evidence is good enough to answer. Two structural issues motivate the design. First, naturally occurring web questions are too easy for training: a model can answer many of them from parametric memory. Second, the team argues, and this is the through-line of the whole report, that inference-time context management is worth more on these benchmarks than most of the reported differences between systems. Long search sessions exhaust the context window before the agent has resolved all its constraints, so the effective search budget ends up much smaller than the nominal context length. Reporting scores under one context-management strategy can therefore obscure how much of the performance is the policy and how much is the surrounding harness.

The data pipeline starts from the hyperlink structure of a web corpus, modeled as a directed graph where pages are nodes and hyperlinks are edges. From a seed page, the system expands along out-links into a local subgraph, then distills that subgraph into a compact connected entity graph of salient entities and typed relations that preserve the cross-page link structure. Over that graph, a generator authors a question and a reasoning path whose traversal uniquely yields the target answer, with a hard constraint that the path spans at least N coupled relations. The crucial step is anchor abstraction: every non-answer entity gets rewritten into a descriptive reference that uniquely identifies the entity without containing its name or aliases. This removes the shortcut where an agent could solve the question by searching a surface string; the resulting question demands disambiguation by reasoning rather than string matching.

Verification uses two criteria judged by a reference model. The difficulty criterion checks that the model fails the question closed-book, which discards anything answerable from memory. The solvability criterion checks that the model solves the question once the supporting entity graph is supplied as context, which discards questions whose answer is wrong or non-unique. Only questions passing both are kept, each paired with a verified unique answer. That is a clean design for scaling training data, though note that the reference model defines the difficulty band, so the pipeline inherits its biases about what is hard.

Trajectories come from a strong teacher solving each question under ReAct against live search tools, with each retrieved page compressed on the fly into a short query-relevant digest so trajectories stay within a bounded context budget. Filtering happens at two levels. At the trajectory level, a rollout must terminate successfully, be judged correct against the reference answer, be non-degenerate, and contain at least K tool-call turns. The degeneracy detector is a sliding-window compression ratio: repetitive text compresses far more than fluent text, so any window whose ratio crosses a threshold signals a loop, whatever its period. At the turn level, an LLM judge labels individual assistant turns keep or mask, with a rubric induced from the data rather than hand-written: the team samples trajectories, asks the judge to critique them in free form, consolidates the recurring failure modes into an explicit rubric, and uses that in the final judging prompt. At most 10 percent of assistant turns in any trajectory get masked, and masked turns remain in the context but are excluded from the training loss.

The reinforcement learning stage optimizes the policy against live search with group-relative policy gradients, using the open-source Relax framework as the training engine. Two implementation details stand out. The first is request-level partial rollout: long-horizon rollouts have a heavy tail, and instead of discarding unfinished sessions, the system aborts in-flight sessions once a step has committed enough completed trajectories and resumes them at the next step from their committed prefix. Rollouts are organized as a forest of message states, each caching its tokens, loss mask, log probabilities, and weight-version deltas, so a resumed trajectory splices prefixes generated under different policy weights, with the mismatch corrected by truncated importance sampling. The cost is roughly two times oversampling as headroom. The second detail is that the reward judge and the observation summarizer are the same model, an in-house 397 billion parameter mixture-of-experts served in FP8 inside the training cluster, which removes any external API dependency from the training loop. The reward is a single binary verdict on the extracted answer against the reference, with no additive format term.

The two stages are alternated in what the team calls SFT-RL climbing. Each RL round explores the current policy; queries with a pass rate between zero and one-half are selected, targeting cases that are solvable but not yet reliable, and among successful rollouts the shortest valid trajectory meeting a minimum tool-call depth is distilled back into the policy through supervised fine-tuning before the next RL round. Because the difficulty band is defined by the current policy's pass rate, it automatically shifts toward harder examples as the policy improves, giving a self-paced curriculum and a natural stopping signal.

The evaluation is the part worth taking most seriously. Every benchmark is run with and without context management, holding the tool set, context limit, and judge fixed, pass at 1, no sub-agents, no test-time verification. With the discard-all strategy enabled, Iris-mini scores 82.2 on BrowseComp, 84.8 on BrowseComp-ZH, 86.9 on DeepSearchQA, and 52.3 on Humanity's Last Exam text-only; Iris-pro scores 88.6, 85.1, 92.9, and 56.4. That is the strongest overall result among open-source search agents in each parameter range, ahead of comparable 35 billion models like XYZ-Aquila-mini and Apodex 1.0-mini on BrowseComp, and ahead of 397 billion class systems like Nex N2-Pro and MiroThinker-1.7 on most of the four. Frontier systems still lead on some columns: Claude Fable 5 reaches 94.2 on DeepSearchQA and 64.5 on HLE, and Apodex 1.0-H reaches 94.4 on DeepSearchQA.

The context-management ablations are where the report earns its credibility. For Iris-mini, management is worth up to 21.2 points on BrowseComp, against at most 12.8 on BrowseComp-ZH, 9.2 on HLE, and 8.9 on DeepSearchQA. The ordering tracks how often a session actually runs out of context: BrowseComp-style tasks are long-horizon information seeking where the accumulated tool history becomes the binding constraint, while HLE is expert reasoning where retrieval is complementary, so extending the effective search horizon recovers less there. The authors also note that adding retry on top of discard-all pushes Iris-pro above 90 on BrowseComp but at substantial extra inference cost, and they deliberately report the discard-all numbers as primary.

Skeptically, a few things to re-verify before building on this. The reward model, the observation summarizer, and the trajectory judge are all in-house models whose identities and quality are not fully specified, and the whole training loop depends on them, so independent reproduction will hinge on the released components actually matching what was used. The difficulty filter is defined by one reference model's closed-book failures, which can encode that model's blind spots into the training distribution. And the benchmark itself has known annotation problems: the paper documents a BrowseComp-ZH case where their answer Bolton is directly supported by the source material but the official ground truth is Lannister. The honest read is that this is a solid recipe report with a well-controlled evaluation, the strongest open search-agent numbers in these ranges today, with the caveat that harness effects are large enough that cross-paper comparisons should always be made under identical context-management settings.
