WorldSculpt: Generating Compositional Worlds from Grounded Videos, by Muyao Niu, Jixuan He, and Ruihan Yu, from Alaya Lab and the University of Tokyo. The problem is generating a compositional 3D representation of a cluttered real scene containing hundreds of objects, where the output is a collection of individual object meshes placed in a shared world frame, rather than a fused mesh or a point cloud of Gaussians. The motivation is practical: downstream applications like gaming, AR and VR, simulation, and robotics do not consume an undifferentiated soup of surface geometry. They need each chair and vase to be a separate mesh that can be selected, moved, and re-simulated on its own.

The tension in the prior work is clean to state. Geometry-centric methods, per-scene optimization, feed-forward volumetric or Gaussian regressors, pointmap models, recover accurate geometry in the observed regions but reconstruct the scene as a single fused representation and leave incomplete geometry wherever occlusion hides it. Generative priors offer the complementary strength: they learn a distribution over plausible 3D content and can infer coherent geometry beyond the visible evidence, but existing ones either process the whole scene jointly and still output a monolithic representation, or model a complete individual object from a single pose-free image, which does not jointly condition on a consistent set of views or generate in a known scene coordinate frame. The paper's claim is that a complex scene with hundreds of objects can be generated compositionally by adapting a strong single-object 3D generative prior to multi-view observations.

The method, instantiated with the Pixal3D single-object prior, works in three steps. First, for each object, an anchor-aligned canonical frame is constructed from the scene observations, and per-view DINOv3 features are lifted into the corresponding canonical voxel volume. Second, the multi-view features are fused with an IBR-style aggregator that is permutation-invariant and supports a variable number of inputs; the aggregated 3D condition is injected into the frozen prior through zero-initialized projection layers, while low-rank adapters adapt the pretrained network to exploit the extra multi-view evidence. Third, each object is generated as an individual mesh in its canonical frame and placed into the scene through its canonical-to-world transformation, with no cross-object fusion and no joint shape optimization. The load-bearing claim is that the prior is finetuned entirely on individual objects in canonical space and still generalizes at test time to large scenes with hundreds of densely occluded objects, without any scene-level training. There is also a conditioning-view augmentation curriculum to improve robustness to the partial and degraded observations that cluttered scenes produce.

The inputs are a set of RGB images with instance-level 2D masks and 3D bounding boxes, which the paper treats as available from standard segmentation and object-level localization pipelines, so the system deliberately does not own the front end. That is a reasonable scope decision, but it means the quality of the output inherits the quality of the masks and boxes.

The benchmark contribution is UE-MeshyScene, six large-scale environments rendered in Unreal Engine 5.8: an aircraft hangar, an abandoned-city interior, an old cathedral, an architectural-visualization office, a Japanese school, and a desert town. Each scene contains between 93 and 701 objects, 2,299 overall, rendered along a smooth camera trajectory at 2560 by 1440, producing 265 to 1,758 views per scene, 5,964 in total. Every frame ships with the RGB image, camera pose, per-object instance masks, per-object 3D bounding boxes, and a metric depth map, and because every asset is a known model placed with a known transformation, the dataset has complete per-object mesh ground truth, which is the part that is hard to get from real captures.

The numbers. On the single-object canonical-space stress test, 500 Toys4k objects with 1 to 16 input views and per-view occlusion up to 75 percent, the multi-view model matches the dedicated single-view Pixal3D pipeline when only one view is available, which suggests the adaptation largely preserves the original generative capability, and it outperforms the single-view baselines as more views arrive. The advantage grows sharply under occlusion: with 16 views, performance stays nearly flat up to 50 percent per-view occlusion, while the single-view baselines, and even their own single-view setting, degrade substantially.

On compositional scenes the gaps are larger. On HouseCat6D, a real-capture tabletop benchmark, WorldSculpt reports a Chamfer distance, l2, of 0.28 against the next-best ShapeR at 1.26, and an F-Score of 0.995 against 0.973. On the synthetic Toys4k-Scene clutter, it posts 0.61 against ShapeR at 8.38. On their own UE-MeshyScene, the six cluttered environments, it reports a mean Chamfer l2 of 2.48 against ShapeR at 7.42, with a per-instance median of 0.25, and an F-Score of 0.951 against 0.813. The per-instance medians matter more than the means here, because they say that for a typical object in these scenes the error is tiny, and the means are being pulled up by a minority of difficult instances. The compositional protocol deliberately skips per-object ICP alignment, transforming each mesh into the world frame with its canonical-to-world transform before comparing, so placement error is measured alongside geometry.

Two things to re-verify before adopting this. First, the single strongest baseline comparison on their own benchmark is against ShapeR alone, a single method, and the other compositional baselines, SceneGen, SceneMaker, RecGen, SAM3D, were not run on UE-MeshyScene, only on the easier multi-object sets. That means the headline clutter result has a thin competitive field. Second, the whole pipeline depends on upstream instance masks and 3D boxes, and the paper does not measure how error in those propagates, so on real captures where the front end is noisier than the Unreal renders, the effective quality is unknown. The honest read is that this is a credible demonstration that an object-level generative prior, finetuned only on single objects, can be lifted to compositional scene generation of genuinely cluttered scenes with per-object meshes, and that the multi-view conditioning plus augmentation curriculum is what makes it robust, with the caveat that the benchmark is synthetic, the competitive field on it is narrow, and the front-end localization is out of scope.

The ablations point at where the robustness actually comes from. The single changed component is the fusion module: the learned IBR aggregator versus a plain arithmetic mean of the per-view feature grids. On the sparse, well-observed HouseCat6D scenes the two are similar, because there is little to reweight. As clutter increases the learned aggregator wins consistently, with its largest gains on UE-MeshyScene, where it improves every reported metric over mean fusion, cutting the l2 Chamfer distance by 12 percent and lifting the F-Score from 0.944 to 0.951. The pattern is exactly what you would want: the learned view weighting buys you nothing when objects are fully observed and buys the most precisely when they are not.

There is also a useful proof-of-concept application that goes beyond the captured-scene setting. The authors take a world generated by the Marble 3D Gaussian splatting model, render a sequence of posed RGB and depth observations from it, run SAM3 to recover per-object mask tracks, merge fragmentary tracklets using temporal mask overlap and 3D box consistency, back-project the masked pixels through the known cameras to get coarse 3D boxes, and then feed the standard WorldSculpt pipeline, unmodified, to convert the generated 3DGS world into a compositional scene of per-object meshes. This matters because it means the method can act as a post-hoc converter for existing generative world models: a fused, uneditable 3DGS environment becomes a set of selectable, re-simulatable meshes without retraining the world model. The catch is that the conversion depends on the quality of the rendered depth and the segmentation, which is the same front-end dependency as before, just moved one step upstream.

The verdict, skeptically read: the core demonstration is real and the benchmark is genuinely new, a hundreds-of-object cluttered-scene setting with per-object ground-truth meshes that did not exist before, and the zero-shot generalization from canonical single-object training is the most impressive part. What I would re-verify before adopting this for a production pipeline is the front-end, because the entire method inherits the quality of the video segmentation and camera estimation, the 0.177 CD improvement over UE-MeshyScene is a meaningful but modest margin over the closest prior method on the headline setting, and the 3DGS conversion route is a proof of applicability rather than a validated production path. The pipeline is the part worth stealing, and UE-MeshyScene is the part worth evaluating your own compositional system against.
