Good evening, welcome back to Papercast. I'm your host, and tonight's fifth paper is Recursive Code World Models, from Zhiqi Li, Yuxuan Liao, and Bo Zhu at the Georgia Institute of Technology. This is a 3D generation paper, and it comes out of a trend I have been covering a lot this year: the idea that a 3D world is better represented not as a cloud of points or a mesh, but as an executable program, a piece of code that, when you run it, renders the scene.

Let me frame the problem. There is now a family of methods, sometimes called code world models, that take a single reference image and produce a 3D world expressed as code, typically in something like Three.js or Blender. The appeal of code is real: it is editable, you can reuse components, you can hand it to a physics simulator, and you can inspect the geometry directly rather than reverse-engineering it from rendered pixels. But there has always been a gap between the representation and the construction. Knowing that code is a good way to represent a world does not tell you how to actually build a complex one from a single image. That is the gap this paper addresses.

The core idea is to make the construction process itself recursive. The authors define a representation they call a Recursive Scene Program, an RSP, which represents the executable world as compositional scene code, with parts that can call other parts. And they pair it with a construction solver that recursively calls itself. Every call to the solver follows the same complete process, no matter what scale you are at. First, establish the whole: build a first-pass version of the entire scene or sub-scene you are responsible for. Second, recursively reconstruct the unresolved parts: identify the components that are still too rough, and for each one, make a recursive call to the very same solver, giving it a crop of the reference image and a cropped camera view that is aligned to the parent's. Third, revisit the whole: when the children return their refined sub-programs, compose them back into the parent and do another round of inspection and editing.

That last step, the parent revisitation, is the subtle part. If you only refine the local parts and then stitch them together, you can end up with a scene where every part looks great in isolation but the parts do not fit together: a sign ends up covering a neighboring window, a bridge meets the ground at the wrong height, a shared wall is built twice at slightly different angles. The parent revisitation step exists specifically to catch and fix those composition errors, because they only become visible once the pieces are assembled. So the recursion is not just a divide-and-conquer trick; it is a divide-and-conquer-plus-integration loop, and the integration is where the scene-wide consistency comes from.

A second design detail worth calling out is the camera handling. The reference image is a single 2D projection, so the absolute 3D scale and the hidden geometry are fundamentally ambiguous. What the authors do to make local inspection possible is propagate a shared camera projection across the recursion levels. When a child call is made, it inherits the parent's camera pose and receives a crop of the reference image that uses the same coordinates and the same magnification as the region it is reconstructing. That alignment is what lets the agent, a vision-language coding model, directly compare the reference crop with the rendered crop of its own work and tell whether it is on track.

For the actual agent, the authors drive it with OpenAI's gpt-6-astra through the Codex command-line interface at high reasoning effort, and each node of the recursion runs as its own Codex session. The agent's job at every level is the same: look at the reference, look at the render, edit the program, re-render, repeat, and decide whether to descend deeper or return to the parent.

The results. They evaluate on ten reference scenes, five whole scenes and five local crops, using images from the isometric-city sprite pack and from WorldClaw demonstration renders. They compare against three baselines, SEIG, VIGA, and img2threejs, all driven by the same base model and reasoning effort, which is a fair setup. RCWM gets the highest PSNR and the lowest LPIPS on every one of the ten references, and the highest SSIM on nine of the ten. The numbers are not huge in absolute terms, PSNR in the 17 to 19 range on the whole scenes, but the consistency across the whole set is what stands out, and the gap to the next-best method is often several PSNR points. The one exception the paper itself calls out is the valley-village crop, where VIGA edges RCWM on SSIM, 0.41 to 0.38, which is a scene with enough natural terrain that the code-based reconstruction apparently struggles.

The paper also includes two kinds of evidence that the metrics alone cannot show. The first is a set of additional camera views: the delivered programs are rendered from rotated, elevated, and close-up cameras with no further optimization, which demonstrates that the programs encode real 3D structure and not just a front-facing appearance. The second is the recorded call trees, the actual tree of recursive solver calls made during construction, showing nested calls to deeper subworlds and the returns back up to their parents. Those traces are the direct evidence that the recursion is happening as described, and they are also the most useful artifact for anyone who wants to reproduce or build on the method. The cleanest way to think about the contribution is that it reframes world building as a search problem over a hierarchy of subproblems, and then hands the whole hierarchy to a reasoning agent that already knows how to iterate, rather than asking a single model to produce everything in one forward pass.

The ablation is the more interesting result. They compare five workflows: a flat single-process build with zoom tools, a local-then-global build, a global-then-local build, a recursive solver restricted to two levels, and the full recursive solver with free depth. On the medieval-village scene, the free-depth recursion expands to three levels with 33 nodes, and it lifts the whole-frame PSNR from 16.8 to 19.0 and the local detail-window SSIM from 0.52 to 0.60 relative to the fixed two-level variant, while beating all three non-recursive workflows. That is direct evidence that the deeper recursive calls are doing real work on fine-scale detail, and not just reorganizing the same amount of effort.

The skeptical read. First, the evaluation set is small: ten scenes, single runs, one base model. The authors are honest about this in their limitations section, and they note that convergence of the iterative refinement is not guaranteed, which is a real practical concern for a system whose cost scales with how deep the recursion goes and how many refinement rounds each node takes. Second, single-view reconstruction leaves hidden geometry and absolute scale genuinely ambiguous, so the program encodes what is visible plus the model's best assumptions about what is not, and there is no metric in the paper that measures how good those hidden-side assumptions are. Third, all of the wins are measured against the reference camera; the rotated and elevated views they show look plausible, but there is no quantitative evaluation of the unseen side of the scene, which is where a code world model should really be tested.

Two-sentence verdict. Recursive Code World Models is a clean, well-motivated contribution: a global-local-global recursive solver with parent revisitation that beats prior code-based image-to-scene methods across the board and shows, by ablation, that deeper recursive calls improve fine-scale fidelity. The small, single-run, single-model evaluation keeps this at proof-of-concept strength, but the recursive construction principle is a genuinely reusable idea for anyone building code-based world models.
