Motion-Omni: End-to-End Joint Speech and Full-Body Motion for Spoken Dialogue, by Chengqian Ma, Wei Tao, and Haoyu Zhang, from Peking University, LIGHTSPEED, and the Chinese University of Hong Kong Shenzhen, with the corresponding author as an independent researcher. The question this paper asks is whether full-body motion can be a native output of a spoken dialogue model, generated from the same hidden states that produce the speech, instead of the standard cascade where a dialogue model first generates the response and a separate motion model then runs a second full inference pass over the finished audio. The cascade has two structural costs: the second pass, and the fact that no motion objective can ever update the speech or dialogue parameters.

The framework has four components: a Speech Projector, an LLM backbone, a Speech Generator, and a Part-Aware Motion Generator. The speech pathway is conventional in spirit, an LLM that emits discrete speech units at 12.5 hertz, passed through a generator that renders them to a waveform. The interesting interface is between the speech generator and the motion generator. The motion generator attends to the speech generator's hidden states as keys and values, and consumes the embeddings of the emitted speech tokens as queries, interpolated from the 12.5 hertz speech-unit rate up to the 30 hertz motion rate. Motion is thus generated directly from the representations that produce the speech, with no rendered waveform as an intermediate input, and it covers explicit facial expression plus hand, upper-body, and lower-body motion. The rate mismatch between the two outputs is the core architectural problem, and this dual-input conditioning is how it is bridged.

The speech pathway itself builds on existing components: a discrete 12.5 hertz speech tokenizer in the style of GLM-4-Voice, and a CosyVoice-style flow-matching decoder. The backbone in the released instantiation, Motion-Omni-Q7, is Qwen2.5-7B-Instruct. Training is a four-stage progression, automatic speech recognition, then text-to-speech, then a text-to-speech-with-motion curriculum, and finally a joint mixture, that co-adapts the Speech Projector, LLM, Speech Generator, and Motion Generator end-to-end while keeping each as a distinct module. The ablation the team leans on is important: with the speech pathway frozen, the generated motion remains visibly misaligned with the audio. Joint training is not optional in this design. Motion supervision is what preserves motion-relevant timing and contextual cues in the shared states, while speech supervision constrains those states to keep their spoken-dialogue ability. The two outputs live at heterogeneous rates and share parameters through which the two losses can interfere, which is precisely why the architecture and the curriculum have to be designed together rather than bolted on.

The supervision problem is scale. Captured audiovisual corpora record many speakers and voices, but spoken dialogue models are trained toward a consistent target voice, so their motion supervision should be paired with responses in that same voice. Existing corpora cannot supply that at scale. The solution is teacher pseudo-labeling: a replaceable motion teacher, the LOM model in this instantiation, labels every response waveform, and a dual-metric quality score ranks the generated supervision and induces the training curriculum. Applied to the InstructS2S-200K dataset, this yields 422,856 teacher-generated pseudo-labeled speech-motion samples, about 1,402 hours. The trade the authors make explicit is that they accept the teacher as a quality ceiling in exchange for scale and voice consistency, which is why the quality ranking of the synthetic labels is essential rather than a nicety.

For evaluation they release SwDA-500 and, to their knowledge, the first public protocol for stochastic open-ended full-body spoken dialogue. The key design choice is matched audio: the same generated response audio is fed to each motion system, so differences in response content and prosody do not confound the motion comparison, while a shared renderer, reference-aware automatic metrics, paired human evaluation, and a common latency protocol cover the full output.

The results. On the reference-free motion metrics, Motion-Omni-Q7 matches the same-audio teacher cascade to within 2 percent while running 5.4 times faster, with a real-time factor of 0.78, meaning it responds faster than real time. The matched cascade, which uses the same speech model plus the LOM teacher as a separate audio-to-motion stage, takes 23.35 seconds per response against 4.32 seconds for the end-to-end model. Among the systems that do not run the motion teacher at inference time, it posts the highest beat correlation and diversity, and a word error rate of 2.62 percent, the lowest among the omni-modal systems compared. The quality-latency plot is the clearest statement: the two cascade baselines each approach the end-to-end model on one axis, one on speed and one on beat correlation, but neither does so on both.

The human evaluation is smaller and should be read accordingly. Four annotators rated 25 paired clips per arm on rhythm, semantic alignment, and body naturalness, with majority vote. Against the EMAGE cascade with matched audio, the pooled margin is plus 25, 45 wins, 10 ties, 20 losses. Against the LOM teacher with matched audio it is 25 wins, 27 ties, 23 losses, with ties the most frequent verdict, and the authors themselves note that with four annotators and 25 pairs they do not read the smaller margins as reliable preference signals. They attribute the preference over the cascades partly to a domain mismatch: the baselines were trained on corpora that include monologue and public-speaking, so their motion sometimes shows presentation-style behaviors, pacing, turning, exaggerated arm swings, that read as unnatural in a two-party dialogue.

The limitations are stated plainly and are the right ones to weigh. Motion quality is bounded by the LOM vector-quantized codebook and the teacher's pseudo-labels, so motion outside that distribution cannot be expressed. Only the 7 billion instance was trained; stronger backbones and continuous motion heads are future work. The model does not condition on speaker identity or emotion, the training data is entirely in English, and the system is an offline response generator: it ingests the full user utterance before emitting the first token, so it is not a streaming interaction model. The automatic metrics, FID and beat correlation, are acknowledged as imperfect proxies for perceived naturalness. There is also a documented negative result: a video-input LLM-as-judge, after five rounds of prompt iteration on a 26-clip calibration set, failed to calibrate against human ratings on this task and was dropped. That is the kind of honest negative that makes the rest of the claims more credible. The bottom line is a clean, well-controlled demonstration that full-body co-speech motion can be a native output of a spoken dialogue model with matched quality and a large latency win, with the caveat that the quality ceiling is set by the pseudo-labeling teacher and the human evidence is still thin.

One implementation detail that matters for anyone reproducing this is the dual-metric quality score used to rank the pseudo-labels. The first metric, a weighted VQ-VAE reconstruction error, measures label fidelity: whether the discrete motion codebook reliably represents the teacher's motion. The second is a beat correlation score, which measures speech-motion rhythmic coupling. It extracts a body-level kinematic envelope on the upper body at 30 hertz, detects kinematic beats as local minima of that velocity above a relative threshold, extracts audio onsets, and scores how well each onset is followed by a motion beat within a Gaussian kernel. A label can pass the fidelity check and still be rhythmically misaligned with the speech; the beat correlation catches exactly that failure mode. The curriculum exposes the network to high-fidelity, well-aligned samples first and works down the ranking, which is the mechanism by which the synthetic supervision stays usable at all.

It is also worth noting the scale of the latency comparison in context. The full Qwen2.5-Omni-plus-LOM cascade takes 99 seconds per response, a real-time factor of 18.34, and Qwen2.5-Omni-plus-EMAGE takes 48.65 seconds. The matched-audio comparison is the fair one, because it isolates the motion pathway, but the headline 5.4 times figure comes from that matched comparison against the LOM cascade at 23.35 seconds. SwDA-500 itself is derived from the public Switchboard Dialog Act Corpus by a stated filtering procedure, and all source corpora are public, which helps reproducibility.
