Show-Harness, presenting a way to let a single vision-language model drive a real robot without any fine-tuning. Lead contributors from Show Lab at the National University of Singapore, with Yanzhe Chen, Zechen Bai, and Zhijun Cao as co-first authors. The core claim is that the bottleneck in robot manipulation is not the model's intelligence but the interface between the model's semantic world and the robot's physical actuators. If you design that interface right, a frontier VLM can pick up a banana and put it on a plate with zero training data.

The problem they are attacking is a real tension in the field. Vision-language-action models, the dominant paradigm, fine-tune a VLM to regress continuous robot actions. That works, but it collapses the model's broad semantic knowledge into an opaque pixel-to-motor mapping. Every new robot arm, every new task family, every new environment requires fresh trajectories and retraining. Hierarchical systems go the other direction: the VLM emits subtasks or programs, but a separate controller handles the physical execution, which severs the direct link between what the model intends and what the robot actually does. Show-Harness sits in the middle. It defines a compact set of discrete semantic action units, things like move forward, move right, grasp, release, rotate clockwise. Each unit is one small, bounded motion. The VLM picks the unit; a deterministic embodiment-specific interpreter maps it to a concrete joint-space trajectory. The VLM stays responsible for fine-grained physical decisions, but it never has to predict a continuous action vector.

There are two deployment modes. In zero-shot mode, they plug in a closed-source frontier model, Gemini 3.1 Pro with medium thinking effort, and it just works on a real Franka arm with no fine-tuning at all. In fine-tuned mode, they take a two-billion-parameter open-source model, Qwen 3.5 2B, and fine-tune it on a few GPU-hours of demonstrations collected through their own GUI-based data-collection tool called GUMI. GUMI is a browser-based interface where a human or an agent clicks buttons to issue the same semantic action units, so the demonstrations are collected in exactly the same action space the policy will use at inference. No teleoperation hardware, no specialized controllers.

The results are strong. On cross-task evaluation across ten object-to-receptacle tasks, zero-shot Gemini 3.1 Pro averages 89 percent success, and the fine-tuned 2B model hits 86 percent. The strongest VLA baseline, RATS, manages 57 percent; the next best, H-VLA, gets 50 percent. Cross-environment, where they shift background, lighting, viewpoint, and add distractors, zero-shot Gemini hits 100 percent, and the fine-tuned model gets 88 percent. Cross-embodiment, transferring between a seven-degree-of-freedom Franka and a six-degree-of-freedom AgileX arm, zero-shot scores 93 percent and fine-tuned scores 87 percent. The sim-to-real result is the most striking: the fine-tuned model, trained entirely on simulated demonstrations collected via GUMI, succeeds 13 out of 20 times on the real robot, while every trainable VLA baseline scores zero.

So how does a two-billion-parameter model get close to a frontier model's performance? The answer is in the loop, not the scale. After every action unit executes, the harness reorganizes the multi-view camera feed and proprioceptive state into a fresh perceptual context and feeds it back. The VLM reasons over the consequence of its last move before choosing the next one. That per-step feedback is what lets a small model recover from small errors instead of compounding them into a failed trial. It also supports subtask-level recovery: when the gripper misses, the model can re-plan the approach rather than blindly repeating the same trajectory.

The semantic action space itself is deliberately boring, and that is the point. There are roughly a dozen units: directional translations in six axes, two gripper actions, and two rotations. The interpreter for each embodiment clamps each unit to a safe step size and applies safety limits, so a frontier model that hallucinates an aggressive action still only produces a bounded, physically plausible motion. The same ten or so units work on both arms; switching embodiments is a config change in the interpreter, not a retrain. That is why the cross-embodiment numbers hold up: the policy never learned arm-specific motor programs, it learned to pick the right semantic unit given what it sees. This is also a safety property, and the paper leans on it. A frontier model's failures in a closed API are usually hallucinated instructions, but here a hallucinated instruction can only map to one of a dozen safe, bounded motions. The blast radius of a bad token is a single small step, not an unbounded trajectory.

Where the approach shows its limits is the tasks that resist semantic decomposition. The fine-tuned model's weakest rows are the smaller, fiddlier objects: a tennis ball into a plate drops to 8 out of 10 even for zero-shot Gemini, and a small chess piece into a bowl is where the gap between the two modes is widest, 9 out of 10 for fine-tuned against 10 for zero-shot on the plate variant. Precision matters more when the object is small, and a bounded discrete step gives you coarser control than a continuous action chunk can. The paper is honest that the interface trades some raw precision for generality, and their capability analysis section probes exactly where physical and semantic adaptability break down.

GUMI deserves a moment, because it solves a practical problem that has slowed VLA research for years: collecting robot data. The standard pipeline requires a teleoperation rig, a human physically moving a master arm to drive the robot, which is expensive, slow, and embodiment-specific. GUMI replaces that with a browser page showing the robot's camera feed and a row of buttons for the semantic action units. A human clicks forward, right, grasp, release, and the interpreter executes each as a bounded motion while the cameras record. The result is a demonstration in the exact action space the policy learns, with no hardware. Because the interface is the same across arms, a demonstration collected for one embodiment can inform a policy that transfers to another. The authors also show agents can drive GUMI, which opens a path to auto-collected demonstration data without human labeling in the loop.

The capability analysis is the part that most deserves a skeptical read. The cross-environment numbers are near perfect, but the environment shifts are the ones a VLM is already good at: different backgrounds, lighting, and viewpoints do not change the manipulation dynamics. The sim-to-real result is stronger, because the fine-tuned model has only ever seen simulated pixels and still lands 13 of 20 real-world trials, but the task set is the same ten object-to-receptacle transfers, which are the easiest manipulation problems you can define. Long-horizon tasks with tool use, deformable objects, or force-dependent contacts are not in scope. The interface's generality claim is real but bounded: it generalizes across the tested task, environment, and embodiment dimensions, and the mechanism for that generalization, a stable semantic action space plus per-step feedback, is well understood. What is not yet shown is how the unit set scales as tasks get harder, and whether a frontier model's semantic reasoning stays reliable when the physical margin for error is thin.

The broader bet is that frontier VLMs keep getting smarter about the world, and if the interface is stable, every improvement in the model transfers to the robot for free. You do not retrain a policy when a better vision-language model ships; you swap the endpoint. That is a fundamentally different scaling story from VLAs, where progress requires more robot data. On the tasks they test, the interface clearly does the job, and the zero-shot result with a closed model is the strongest argument that semantic reasoning, not motor regression, is where manipulation capability actually lives. The real test will be whether this holds when the task set stops being pick-and-place, but the direction is hard to argue against.
