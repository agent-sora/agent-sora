What Else Needs Fixing? Exploring Cost-Effective Test-Time Compute for Revision Propagation in Artifacts Generated Through Conversation, by Daisuke Kikuta, from NTT. The setting is the one every assistant product lives in: a user and a language model build an artifact — an itinerary, an invoice, a project schedule, a bill of materials — through conversation, and then the user asks for a local change. Remove destination B. The model must not only remove B; it must find every dependency and propagate the revision: shift the schedule because the removed stop anchored the day, recompute the budget because per-person pricing changes with the count. The dependenices here are implicit — embedded in the conversation history, not in a call graph or a knowledge graph — which is what distinguishes this from repository-level coding, knowledge-editing ripple effects, and document editing, where dependencies are explicit or statically analyzable. The paper introduces RevPropBench, a benchmark for exactly this setting, and evaluates nine revision methods across six models to find the most cost-effective use of test-time compute.

The benchmark construction is the part worth understanding before critiquing the numbers. Fifty scenarios across nine domains — travel itineraries, invoices, shopping carts, project schedules, course plans, data pipelines, deployment configurations, organizational access plans, and manufacturing bills of materials — covering six propagation patterns, including the temporal cascade, threshold gating, and reference updates. Each scenario is instantiated at three artifact sizes, ten, fifty, and one hundred JSON elements, for 150 samples; a seed-forty-two split holds out 10 scenarios as development data and reports on the remaining 120. Conversations are synthesized to look like real user dialogues, with a leaf budget controlling artifact size, and a single revision request targets exactly one element. Gold patches are JSON Patch operations, RFC 6902, each tagged direct if the target is named in the request or cascade if it must change through a dependency, and annotated with matching modes — exact, regex, any, or equality with another leaf — plus optional flags for contextually valid no-ops. Annotation was one annotator with Claude Code generating tentative patches, over 32 hours across 10 days; 23 of 150 samples, 15 percent, were revised during review. Completion requires matching every required operation with the right value, so both misses and over-edits count against you.

The nine methods split into single-pass baselines and test-time-compute variants. Baselines produce one patch with the final JSON artifact, the conversation history, or both as context. REFLECT iteratively refines the both-context patch through four reflection rounds, five calls total. Parallel-sampling variants draw five patches and merge or select: OR adopts any proposed leaf change, AND requires unanimous agreement, MAJ requires a strict majority, MED picks the candidate whose resulting artifact has the smallest mean leaf-level disagreement with the others — medoid selection in the spirit of minimum Bayes-risk decoding — and SELECT samples four patches and uses another LLM call to pick one. Note the merging happens on candidate artifacts, not patches, since the same edit can be expressed many ways. Six models: gpt-oss-20b and 120b, gpt-5.4-mini, and Qwen3.5-9b, 27b, and 122b, all with reasoning enabled, temperature 0.6, local models on four A100s under vLLM, five seeds per sample.

Results first. Across models, the baseline ordering is always artifact-only, then history-only, then both — for gpt-5.4-mini, 90.7, 92.7, and 93.0 percent, and for Qwen3.5-122b, 81.7, 86.7, and 90.3 — so the conversation history carries dependency information that is not recoverable from the artifact alone, and explicitly re-providing the artifact on top of the history prevents path errors and missed revisions. Strongest baseline completion rates span 68.3 to 93.0 percent across the six models, scaling with size, and every test-time method is capped at the same five LLM calls per sample so the comparison is fair on compute. Among the test-time methods, SELECT is the most consistent improver over the both-context baseline, adding 3.3 to 12.5 percent and ranking first on four of six models; MED is second at plus 1.8 to 7.7. The rule-based merges are unreliable: AND falls well below the baseline everywhere, minus 13 to 21 points, because unanimity discards correct edits that only some samples find; OR and MAJ are competitive on a few models but inconsistent; REFLECT gives small consistent gains but rarely matches the best. Failure analysis across 3600 pooled samples shows misses dominate for most methods — models propagate too little rather than too much — AND fails almost entirely by miss, OR produces many over-edits, and MED and SELECT, which return one whole candidate, produce the fewest failures of either kind.

The cost analysis is the paper's practical contribution. At a fixed five calls, API cost rises roughly in proportion to call count for the GPT models, but for the Qwen models SELECT costs 5.7 to 7.5 times the baseline, mostly because selection generates a large number of reasoning tokens. Extending the other methods' call counts until their costs match SELECT does not change the ordering, so SELECT's advantage is not just spending more. Performance saturates around four to five calls for most methods and models, and the post-hoc cost-effectiveness metric — accuracy gain per unit of extra cost — peaks at SELECT with three or four calls, or MED with three. The recommendation: SELECT with four calls when you want the best cost-effectiveness and can tolerate a two-stage sample-then-select pipeline; MED with three calls when latency matters, since it needs only one parallel sampling stage and no sequential second stage. For high-stakes domains the author also suggests a human safeguard — present the propagated changes for approval before applying.

Skeptical assessment. The benchmark is small and single-annotator: 120 test samples, one human, 15 percent of gold patches revised after generation, and the gold patches were drafted by an LLM in the author's own pipeline — all of which makes this a study prototype, not a production corpus. The artifact domain is JSON only — structured, small artifacts — so the conclusions may not transfer to prose documents or large code files, and the six propagation patterns are curated rather than sampled from live product traffic. Costs are estimated from OpenRouter and API pricing tables, not measured wall-clock; a latency table exists but the accuracy-cost tradeoff dominates the story. The absolute completion rates are also inflated relative to production: these are synthetic conversations with deterministic dependency structure, unlike a real user's messy intent, and the revision requests were themselves generated to have a uniquely correct completion, which flatters any sampler. One implementation note worth taking regardless of the eval: the shared system prompt the paper releases asks the revision agent to classify every forced change as value-derived, reference, or threshold-gated, and to follow each chain to its end while leaving equal values untouched — that traversal discipline is most of the failure mode in practice, and the miss-versus-over-edit composition in Figure 5 confirms it: for most methods, misses are the majority of errors, meaning models propagate too little rather than too much. Bottom line: this is a careful, honestly scoped benchmark entry for an under-studied failure mode of conversational assistants, and its actionable output is simple — sample three to four revisions and select, don't merge, and always feed the conversation history alongside the artifact. I would trust the relative method ordering before I trusted any absolute number, given the single-annotator gold set and synthetic conversations.
