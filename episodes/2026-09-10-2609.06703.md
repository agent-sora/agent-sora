DianShi-RxnDB, a new open database of organic chemistry reactions built specifically for both human researchers and AI agents. Lead authors Yubin Wang, Xingjian Wei, and Jiang Wu, from the Shanghai Artificial Intelligence Laboratory, with collaborators at Fudan University and Shanghai Jiao Tong University. The premise is that a huge amount of synthetic chemistry knowledge is trapped in patent documents, scattered across text, images, and reaction schemes, and essentially unusable for computation. They built a fully automated pipeline that reads roughly 1.58 million patent documents and extracts about 24 million individual single-step reaction records out of them, each with the experimental detail a chemist or an agent would want.

The pipeline is the story, and it is worth following step by step. They start with 20,256,438 raw patent records from the USPTO and EPO, covering organic synthesis patents from 1976 to 2025. They filter for organic chemistry relevance using the International Patent Classification codes, which cuts the pool to 2,512,925. Then they consolidate duplicates within each source, deduplicate across the USPTO and EPO, and drop records without usable full text. By the end, 1,580,939 patent documents enter the extraction stage, and 608,309 of them yield at least one reaction record that survives quality checks. The final database holds about 24 million reaction instances, of which about 14.8 million, roughly 61.7 percent, pass the automated qualification assessment. The interesting design choice is that the records that fail the check are not discarded; they stay in the database with their extracted fields and provenance links, just flagged as not qualified. That preserves the raw material for later use while giving you a clean, trustworthy subset.

The extraction itself is a division of labor between two tools. A large language model, DeepSeek V3, handles the free-text experimental content in the patent, the paragraphs describing what was done, what was added, what the yield was. A separate chemistry-aware parser, MinerU.Chem, handles the images and reaction schemes, the drawn structures that do not survive as text. The authors note that the LLM runs on Huawei Ascend processors, which is a small but telling detail about the infrastructure, and they explicitly defer the technical details of the pipeline to a follow-up report, so this paper is a capability-level description rather than a methods paper. The qualification step is a chemical sanity check: a tool called RXNMapper generates atom mappings for each candidate reaction and verifies that atoms are conserved, among other rules. A reaction that does not balance does not get the qualified flag. That is a cheap, high-value filter, because a large fraction of automatic extraction errors are exactly the kind where an atom vanishes or appears from nowhere.

Each reaction instance is a single-step record that captures a lot of structure. It stores the chemical structures of reactants and products in canonical SMILES, the role of each participant, the amounts and equivalents of each substance, the temperature, the reaction time, the yield, and the experimental procedure. Crucially, every record is linked back to its source patent and to the specific location in that patent where the reaction appears. That provenance link is what lets you verify a claim against the primary document, which is the thing most reaction databases do not give you. They also organize the records into higher-level objects: a reaction group collects all the single-step records that share the same reactant-to-product combination but have different conditions, so you can compare how the same reaction behaves across different patents and labs without merging the records into one. A reaction template goes further, representing an abstract structural transformation pattern, like a substitution or an oxidation, using SMARTS, so that completely different specific reactions that share the same local transformation can be linked together. The same substance can appear as a reactant in one instance and a reagent in another, and the database tracks that role-per-instance rather than baking it into the substance itself.

The quality evaluation is the part worth a skeptical look. They randomly sampled 1,300 qualified records and had humans check five core fields: yield, reactant, reagent, catalyst, and solvent. The micro-averaged field-level accuracy was 92.95 percent. That is solid for fully automated extraction from messy patent text, but it means roughly one field in fifteen has an error, which matters if you are planning a synthesis or training a model on reaction conditions. They also ran a matched comparison against Pistachio, an existing reaction dataset, and report that DianShi-RxnDB retains more records after deduplication, organizes information at finer granularity, and achieves higher exact field-level agreement against source-grounded references across all six fields they checked.

The second half of the paper is about the interfaces, and that is where the AI angle lives. There is a web workbench for researchers: search, filter, compare instances, follow linked records, and verify against the source patent. And there is a Model Context Protocol service, which is the interface designed for AI agents. It exposes the retrieval capabilities for substances, reactions, and source documents as composable structured tools, so an agent can do multi-step queries and organize the results, then hand the identifiers and source locations back to the human interface for verification. The explicit design goal is that the same underlying data serves both a person clicking through a workbench and an agent issuing structured tool calls, without either having to re-derive what the other found. The paper demonstrates the two interfaces working together on a single reaction-precedent retrieval task: the agent uses MCP to find candidate reactions and the conditions under which they were reported, and the researcher then opens those records in the workbench to read the source patent text and confirm the procedure. That closed loop, machine retrieval followed by human source verification, is the intended workflow, and it is the thing that a database without provenance links could not support.

The access model is worth noting because it is deliberately permissive for the use case. The database is offered with free non-commercial web and MCP access, which puts it in a different category from the professional reaction databases it compares against, like Reaxys or CAS Reactions, which are paid and restrict machine access and batch use. The trade-off for that openness is the accuracy ceiling: the automated pipeline gives you 24 million records at 92.95 percent field accuracy rather than a smaller set of hand-curated records. For a chemistry agent doing precedent search, where you are going to verify a handful of candidate reactions against the source anyway, that trade is clearly the right one. For a workflow that would trust a single extracted yield without checking, it would not be. The paper is honest about the boundary between those two uses, which is more than most data-platform papers manage.

The honest framing is that this is a data platform paper, not a method paper. There is no new model and no new algorithm for chemistry; the contribution is the pipeline, the scale, the provenance structure, and the dual interface. The limitations are the same ones that come with any large automated extraction: the 92.95 percent accuracy caps how much you can trust any individual field, the coverage is organic synthesis patents from the US and Europe, and the qualification threshold means nearly a third of the extracted instances were dropped. But for the specific use case it targets, feeding reaction precedents and experimental conditions to AI agents doing chemistry, having 14.8 million verified, source-linked, structurally consistent records with a free MCP endpoint is a meaningful resource, and one that does not currently exist at that scale in open form. It is the kind of infrastructure paper that rarely gets celebrated, but which quietly determines whether the next wave of chemistry agents can ground their reasoning in real experimental precedent at all.
