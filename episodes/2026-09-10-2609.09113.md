SAEScientist-Bench, a new benchmark for testing whether AI agents can do autonomous interpretability research. Lead authors Yuqiao Tan, Shizhu He, and Jun Zhao, from the Key Laboratory of Cognitive Intelligence at the Chinese Academy of Sciences, with Kang Liu as the senior contributor. The motivation is a gap in the recursive self-improvement story. Most work on autonomous AI research automates the training pipeline: agents curate data, design algorithms, and run post-training. But they treat the model as a black box, judged only by external task scores. That leaves a missing pillar, which is the ability to look inside the model and audit what it actually learned. This benchmark measures that pillar, using Sparse Autoencoders as the tool.

The setup is concrete. The model under test is Gemma 2, 9 billion, with Gemma Scope, a pretrained sparse autoencoder, attached to two layers of its residual stream. The SAE dictionary at each layer has 131,072 features. The agent is given a target concept, like Portuguese, or an earnings report, or a clinical symptom report, and its job is to design contrastive probes, texts that should activate the concept and texts that should not, and use those probes to navigate the dictionary and submit the single feature ID that best represents the concept. The feature is then scored on three axes against an expert reference feature pulled from Neuronpedia. The three axes are activation rank, how prominently the feature fires relative to the whole dictionary; activation selectivity, measured by the area under the receiver operating characteristic curve separating positive texts from contrastive controls; and causal steering, whether adding the feature's direction to the hidden state actually changes the model's generation toward the target.

The benchmark has 20 tasks across concept domains: multilingual understanding, specialized document formats, and safety-critical topics. They evaluate ten frontier agent configurations, running each three independent times per task and averaging. The leaderboard is led by Kimi K3 with a composite overall score of 65.82, closely followed by Claude Opus 5 at 65.41 and Claude Sonnet 5 at 65.04. The expert baseline sits at 85.56. So the top agent is about twenty points behind the human-curated reference, a substantial but not absurd gap.

The most informative result is the split between the three axes. On activation selectivity, the agents are close to expert: Kimi K3 reaches 92.91 against the expert's 98.92. The agents can reliably find features that fire on the target and stay quiet on the controls. But on causal steering, they fall far short. The best steering score is Grok 4.6 at 31.47, against the expert's 57.75, barely half. That is the core finding: finding a feature that is correlated with and selective for a concept is much easier than finding one that is a potent causal lever on generation. A feature can rank highly and separate cleanly on static texts while still doing nothing when you intervene with it.

The behavioral analysis explains why. The authors look at the probes agents actually write and how they read the results, and they find a recurring failure: agents can design informative contrasts to rule out spurious candidates, but they frequently misinterpret the experimental measurements. In the Portuguese case study, one agent submits a feature with a perfect selectivity score of 100 on activation, but its activation rank collapses to 3.97 because the target activation is so weak that the feature barely stands out in the full dictionary. Another agent, Opus 5, writes a probe in English about Portuguese, which should not activate the Portuguese feature, and gets a reading it misreads, landing on a feature with near-zero steering. The pattern is that the agents are better at the search phase than at the validation phase. They generate good hypotheses but struggle to read the data that tells them whether the hypothesis held.

If you look at the leaderboard row by row, the story is consistent across the whole field, not just the top model. The proprietary frontier agents cluster tightly at the top: Kimi K3, Claude Opus 5, and Claude Sonnet 5 all land within a point of each other on the composite, in the 65 range, which tells you the task is hard enough that the gap between the best agents is small compared to the gap between the best agent and the expert reference. The open-weight models sit meaningfully lower, and the spread within the open group is large, which is the pattern you expect when a capability is still emerging rather than saturated. What the tight top cluster does imply is that this is not a single-model trick. Whatever the best agent is doing, several unrelated model families are doing approximately the same thing, so the bottleneck is in the task structure, not in one vendor's training data.

The three-axis split is really the paper's central diagnostic, and it points at a specific, fixable failure rather than a vague "agents are not good at interpretability." Activation selectivity, the ability to find a feature that fires on the target and stays quiet on the controls, is close to solved. The agents get most of the way there, and that part of the job, designing good contrastive texts and reading a receiver operating characteristic curve, is something current agents are genuinely strong at. Causal steering, the ability to find a feature whose direction actually moves generation when you intervene, is the weak axis, and it is weak by a factor of almost two. That gap is the thing a better agent, or a better measurement, or both, has to close before autonomous interpretability research stops being a demonstration and starts being a workflow you can trust. The practical implication is that the next generation of interpretability agents will need stronger experimental reasoning, not just better search. They already know how to find a candidate; what they lack is the discipline to run the intervention, read the steering result honestly, and discard a candidate that only correlates rather than causes.

There is also a technical subtlety the paper surfaces about how to score features at all. They show that using the maximum single-token activation to measure a feature is fragile: a single isolated loanword can spike one token and inflate the score. Averaging the top three token activations instead filters those morphological artifacts and gives a more stable estimate of true semantic activation. That is a small methodological point, but it matters because it changes which features look good on the leaderboard. It also sets a precedent for how future interpretability benchmarks should handle token-level measurement, because the choice between a max and an averaged statistic is not neutral, and a benchmark that bakes in the wrong one will systematically favor or penalize whole families of features.

The honest limits are stated clearly. The benchmark is single-feature discovery in one model, Gemma 2 9B, across two layers and a fixed probe interface. It does not cover multi-feature circuit discovery, other model families, or open-ended hypothesis generation without a pre-specified concept. The evaluation relies on fixed text suites, frozen expert baselines, and an automated judge for steering, all of which introduce some noise. The authors position this as a first standardized foundation, not a complete map of autonomous interpretability, and they are explicit that the point is to establish a reproducible measurement rather than to crown a winning system. But the finding it establishes is real and useful: experimental model understanding, the ability to inspect and causally steer a model's own representations, is a measurable capability, and frontier agents are only partway there. The gap on causal steering is the bottleneck that any closed-loop, white-box autonomous research workflow has to close first, and this benchmark at least gives the field a shared, reproducible number that everyone can close it against.
