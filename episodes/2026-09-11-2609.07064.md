Good evening, welcome back to Papercast. I'm your host, and tonight's fourth paper is SpatialBlock, from Soohyun Ryu, Sohee Kim, and Eunho Yang at KAIST in South Korea. This is a spatial reasoning paper for vision-language models, and it has one of the most honest framings I have seen all week: the authors start from the observation that state-of-the-art vision-language models are bad at something that most humans find easy, and then they build a small, synthetic training set to fix it.

The setup is a demonstration of the gap. The paper leads with a simple block-stacking problem: you are shown a 3D structure made of blocks, and you are asked how it looks from a particular viewing direction, which is a mental rotation and projection task. Humans solve these at near-perfect rates, around 95 percent in their figure. GPT-5 gets about 30 percent. Qwen 2.5 7B gets about 21 percent. That is the motivation in a single chart: the leading models have flawed spatial logic, they cannot even reliably do the basic version of a task that developmental psychology has studied for decades.

The key insight is borrowed from how humans actually learn spatial skills. Developmental psychology has shown that foundational spatial abilities are built through structured manipulative play, and block play specifically is one of the best-studied examples. So instead of the usual approach, which is to scrape real-world 3D scenes and label them with dense geometric annotations using external segmentation and depth models, an expensive and noisy process, the authors generate a synthetic dataset of block-stacking problems. The dataset is called SpatialBlock-15k, and it has 15,000 problems in three categories, each with 5,000 examples.

The three categories map to three distinct spatial abilities. The first is 3D-to-2D projection: given a 3D structure and a viewing direction, predict the 2D silhouette, which requires reconstructing the hidden structure and reasoning about which blocks are occluded. The second is viewpoint transformation: predict how the structure looks after a rotation, which requires preserving the relative positions of all components through the change. The third is structural combination: given two separate structures, predict the result of joining them, which requires identifying the contact interface and simulating the merged geometry.

The second design choice is the clever one, and it is where the paper earns its novelty. Real-world scenes are visually complex, and humans navigate that complexity by anchoring on a salient object and reasoning about everything relative to it. The authors encode that behavior into the dataset by adding controlled color modulation. In the projection task, colors encode depth ordering, so the model has to track front-to-back relationships using color as a cue. In the transformation task, one anchor block gets a distinct color and must keep the same structural role before and after the rotation. In the combination task, overlapping blocks of the same color across the two images mark where the structures connect. So color is not decoration; it is a functional signal that forces the model to do anchor-based reasoning, which is exactly what it needs for real scenes.

Now the training, and here the authors make two defensible methodological choices. They train two variants. The direct variant uses standard supervised fine-tuning, predicting the answer token sequence directly. The reasoning variant first does a LoRA-based supervised fine-tune to establish basic task ability, and then runs reinforcement learning with group relative policy optimization, using a multi-part reward that checks answer correctness, enforces the required chain-of-thought format, and penalizes responses that are too short or too long.

There is a nice negative result in there. The conventional recipe for reasoning-based training is a cold-start phase where you synthesize high-quality reasoning trajectories from a big teacher model, then fine-tune a smaller student on them. The authors skipped that, and their reason is a great data point: even the strongest proprietary models do badly on their own dataset, so there is no good teacher to distill from. They found that LoRA-based initialization, which preserves the model's native chain-of-thought ability, works better than trying to recover reasoning ability from low-quality synthesized trajectories. That is a genuinely useful practical lesson for anyone doing RL on a capability the frontier models themselves do not have.

The results. On their held-out in-domain benchmark, SB-Bench, the trained models score in the 90s, which is expected because that is the training distribution. The interesting numbers are the out-of-domain generalization to real-scene spatial benchmarks. The direct variant on Qwen 3-VL-4B reaches 51.3 on MindCube, which the authors call the best open-source performance on that benchmark, and it beats the prior specialist SpatialLadder by about 2.7 points on the 3B scale. The overall average, which blends in-domain and out-of-domain, goes from a backbone baseline of about 36 to about 43 on the 4B model, and the reasoning variant on 7B gets the highest overall at about 43.5. The gains are real but they are in the single digits, and that is the honest size of this result.

The ablations add two useful data points. First, they decompose the training set by question type, and all three categories, projection, transformation, and combination, contribute to the overall score, with no single category alone matching the full dataset, which supports the design choice of covering all three spatial abilities rather than betting on one. Second, the color-cue extension is not just cosmetic: the paper reports that adding the controlled color modulation, the anchor-based version of the tasks, leads to meaningful improvement on real-scene spatial reasoning performance, which is the whole point of the cue in the first place. In other words, the version of the dataset that forces anchor-based reasoning is the version that transfers, which is exactly what the cognitive motivation predicted.

Now the skeptical assessment. First, the in-domain numbers, the 90s on SB-Bench, are almost meaningless for judging generalization, because the test is the same distribution as the training data. A reader who only saw those numbers would be misled, and a good paper would bury them, which this one does not fully. The second caveat is scale: 15,000 synthetic examples is a small dataset, and the out-of-domain gains, while consistent, are modest. Spatial intelligence is a deep capability, and it would be surprising if 15,000 block problems were the whole answer. The third caveat is that the benchmarks themselves, MindCube, MMSI-Bench, SPBench, are still relatively small and multiple-choice, so there is a risk of overfitting to the benchmark format rather than to genuine spatial understanding.

What I find most valuable is the framing, even if the deltas are small. The field has spent a lot of effort on expensive, annotation-heavy real-scene spatial datasets, and this paper is a clean argument that a small, synthetic, cognitively-motivated dataset can match or beat them on real-scene transfer. If that scaling holds, the implication is big: you do not need to scrape and label the physical world to teach a model about space, you need to teach it the right primitives. Block-stacking is one such primitive, and this is a credible first demonstration. And because the data is synthetic, it is cheap to extend: the same generator, with a few new rules, can produce projection and transformation problems at any difficulty, which makes the dataset a starting point for a curriculum rather than a fixed ceiling.

Two-sentence verdict. SpatialBlock is a compact, well-motivated result showing that 15,000 synthetic block-stacking problems, with color as an anchor cue, can lift a vision-language model's real-scene spatial reasoning to competitive levels without any real-scene annotation. The out-of-domain gains are single-digit rather than transformative, so treat this as a promising proof of concept for a cheaper route to spatial intelligence, not a solved problem.
