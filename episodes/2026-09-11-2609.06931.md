Good evening, and welcome to the final paper of tonight's episode. I'm your host, and this one is CARDEA, from Jia-Jen Lee, Shih-Yen Hou, and Kee Koon Ng, a clinical and AI collaboration at China Medical University Hospital and China Medical University in Taichung, Taiwan. This is a medical imaging paper, and it is the kind of paper that tends to be easy to skim past and hard to overestimate, so I want to be careful about both. The system is a large vision-language model that runs an end-to-end pipeline for interpreting coronary angiography, and its distinguishing feature is that it shows its spatial work.

The clinical context. Invasive coronary angiography, or CAG, is the gold standard for diagnosing coronary artery disease. A catheter is threaded to the heart, contrast is injected, and a series of X-ray videos of the coronary arteries is captured from multiple angles. The problem is that these are 2D projections of a 3D structure, so vessels overlap and foreshorten, and a clinician has to mentally integrate several views of each lesion to judge how bad a narrowing really is. This is genuinely hard, and it is not consistent even among experts: the paper cites a study where three experienced cardiologists reading the same angiograms had an overall agreement of about 77 percent. That means roughly a quarter of the time, two experienced specialists disagree.

The gap in the AI literature. There are two families of prior work, and both have a trust problem. The first family is discriminative: deep models that output a closed-ended label or a set of coordinates. These are consistent and checkable, but they do not produce a clinical narrative, and a cascade of separate models compounds errors across stages. The second family is the newer one, large vision-language models that can draft a free-text report. These sound more clinically useful, but a free-text diagnosis that a cardiologist cannot quickly verify is worse than useless in a high-stakes setting, because it invites the clinician to either trust it blindly or spend more time checking it than reading the images. Recent vision-language attempts at CAG either cannot output bounding boxes at all, or produce boxes in a separate sub-task that is not part of the report.

CARDEA's answer is to make the reasoning auditable by grounding it in spatial evidence. The mechanism is called Chain-of-Box, or CoB, which is a variant of chain-of-thought where the model's reasoning trace includes bounding boxes, rectangular regions of the image that mark the anatomical structures the model is using as evidence for each step. When a cardiologist reads the output, they can look at each box and check whether the model is actually looking at the vessel it claims to be looking at. The boxes turn an opaque text rationale into something a human can audit in seconds.

The pipeline has two passes. The first pass runs single-view inference on each angiographic video in parallel. It selects representative keyframes from the raw video, discarding frames that are non-diagnostic because of poor cardiac alignment or insufficient contrast, and it classifies each retained frame as a left-coronary view, a right-coronary view, or other. A rule-based filter then caps the set at about ten keyframes per study while ensuring both the left and right systems are represented. The second pass does study-level multi-image reasoning over those curated keyframes, producing one of three outputs: a dominance classification, which says whether the left or right coronary system supplies the posterior descending artery; a complexity assessment, which discretizes the SYNTAX score, a standard measure of how complex the overall disease is, into normal-to-intermediate versus high; or a zero-shot report generation over the four major vessel branches.

The training is three stages, and it is trained only on closed-ended tasks from public datasets, which matters for reproducibility. Stage one is supervised fine-tuning on single-view tasks, teaching the model to localize vessels and stenoses with boxes. Stage two is a self-distilled cold start: because hand-annotating chain-of-box reasoning traces is expensive, the untuned base model acts as a teacher, generating the reasoning traces from the stage-one model's boxes plus a textual decision guide, and the model is fine-tuned on those. Stage three is reinforcement learning with verifiable rewards, or RLVR, where the reward checks whether the final answer is correct, and it adds an extra reward for using boxes in the reasoning trace, but only at the study level, because that is where a conclusion drawn from several views actually needs an explanation. Open-ended report generation is deliberately kept out of the RL stage, because free text has no rule-based verifier and would require a reward model, which is prone to reward hacking.

The results, and I want to be precise here because medical claims deserve precision. On in-distribution dominance classification, the dedicated classifier beats CARDEA, which is the honest thing to report and a reminder that a specialist model will still win on its home task. But under domain shift, where the distribution changes, CARDEA draws level with the classifier, at an accuracy of 0.91 with a 95 percent confidence interval from 0.86 to 0.95. On complexity assessment, it is comparable to the two interventional cardiologists, at an accuracy of 0.90, confidence interval 0.82 to 0.97. That cardiologist-comparable number is the headline, and the confidence interval is wide enough that you should not over-read it, but it is a real result.

The most interesting number, though, is the zero-shot report generation. Report generation was never in the training data, and it was evaluated on a fully held-out external cohort. The RLVR stage is the only one that improved it: it raised the vessel-severity macro-F1 from 0.513 for the untuned base model to 0.686, confidence interval 0.664 to 0.707. For context, the always-normal floor, which is what a lazy model that just calls everything normal would score, is 0.312, so the trained model is more than twice the floor. The takeaway the authors draw is that RLVR on verifiable closed-ended tasks surfaced an open-ended reporting ability that supervised imitation did not, which is a genuinely interesting finding about what reinforcement learning with verifiable rewards does to adjacent, untrained capabilities.

The skeptical assessment, and it is substantial for a medical paper. First, the evaluation cohort is external but single, and the paper itself says clinical use requires prospective validation against expert cardiologists, which is the correct and necessary caveat. A retrospective, single-cohort result is not a clinical validation. Second, the cardiologist-comparable complexity number rests on a small effective sample, hence the wide confidence interval, and the two cardiologists are the ceiling, not an independent multi-site ground truth. Third, the zero-shot report F1 of 0.686, while a big jump over the floor, is still well short of the reliability a clinical report needs, and the metric, a vessel-severity macro-F1 parsed out of free text by another model, is itself a layer of interpretation. None of this makes the paper wrong; it makes it a pre-clinical result, which is exactly what it claims to be.

Two-sentence verdict. CARDEA is a well-executed pre-clinical result showing that a vision-language model trained only on closed-ended public data, with chain-of-box grounded reasoning and RLVR, can reach cardiologist-comparable complexity assessment and more-than-double the floor on zero-shot report generation, while exposing auditable spatial evidence. The single-cohort, retrospective evaluation means this is a promising research system, not a validated clinical tool, and that distinction is the whole story of where medical AI actually stands right now.
