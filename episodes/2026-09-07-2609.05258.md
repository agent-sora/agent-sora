Ask Before You Optimize: Dynamic Pre-Formulation Clarification for Interactive Optimization, by Sihan Ge, Yichen Lin, and Chenyu Zhou, from Cardinal Operations and Shanghai Jiao Tong University. This paper carves out a specific failure mode in LLM-based operations research and treats it as a standalone task: the case where a user describes a business problem in natural language but leaves out the facts that actually determine the structure of the optimization model. A user may state capacities and demands without specifying the objective, or give a routing rule without saying whether the time windows are hard or soft. Those omissions are not superficial. In a vehicle-routing example the paper uses, if a user omits whether a courier must return to the origin, an agent that silently assumes a closed tour changes the constraint structure without asking.

The paper's central observation is that most evaluations of LLM optimization agents assume a complete specification and measure whether the agent can solve or express a given model, skipping the earlier question of whether the available information even supports a meaningful formulation. Strong LLM agents, the authors find, often declare readiness while core business facts remain unclarified, or silently fill missing facts with unsupported defaults. The task they define is pre-formulation clarification: determine whether the current public specification is model-ready, and when it is not, recover the missing formulation-critical facts with as little interaction as possible. A fact is formulation-critical if its value can change the structure of the resulting optimization formulation.

The proposed method, InterOPT, is a two-stage framework that separates gap diagnosis from interaction control. The first stage, Dynamic Gap Search, identifies formulation-critical gaps and keeps a cross-turn record of the ones that remain unresolved as the conversation progresses. The second stage, Gap-Guided Action Search, uses the currently unresolved gaps to decide whether to ask a targeted question next or to stop. The design point is that persistent gap tracking is decoupled from action selection, so the system can distinguish between a fact it has already recovered and one it has not, rather than re-deriving the state of the specification from scratch each turn. In the default configuration Stage 1 adds at most three new gaps per turn and Stage 2 instantiates three candidate states and questions on each asking turn, all agent-facing decisions made by the tested model itself.

The evaluation harness, OR-Clarify, is built by taking fully specified optimization tasks and withholding their formulation-critical facts, turning each into a partial public brief plus private, source-supported hidden slots. A fact-bounded simulated user answers only from those private facts and the dialogue history, and never volunteers facts that were not asked for. The benchmark supports both a free-form protocol and a choice-based one, and it scores slot-level recovery, readiness decisions, silent assumptions, and interaction cost. The reported metrics are All-Slot Exact, which requires every hidden slot to be recovered exactly, Core Exact, which counts only cases with at least one top-priority slot, silent assumptions per run, and average turns and average atomic questions. The standard tested model for the method comparisons is DeepSeek V4 Pro, with a maximum of 20 turns and an agent temperature of 0.2, over 100 cases with 5 runs each.

The results are more nuanced than the framing suggests, and the paper is honest about it. In the choice-based setting, InterOPT obtains the highest recovery among the methods evaluated, with an All-Slot Exact of 0.638 and a Core Exact of 0.675, and it leaves the fewest silent assumptions per run, 0.366 against 0.692 for the plain choice baseline. But it does so at a heavy interaction cost, averaging about 10.7 turns and 10 atomic questions, against roughly 3.3 turns for the baseline. The free-form setting is where the method is not clearly best: ORPilot attains the strongest recovery there, GATE remains competitive, and InterOPT is close but not best while still leaving more silent assumptions than the strongest open baselines. The honest summary the authors give is a coverage-cost trade-off: structured gap-guided clarification improves recovery most under constrained answer spaces, but stopping and question efficiency remain limiting factors.

The two-stage ablation is the most useful part for implementation. Removing Stage 1, the gap discovery and ledger, costs the most recovery in both protocols, with the larger reduction in the choice setting, 11.1 percentage points in Core Exact. Removing Stage 2, the gap-to-question action selection, has the opposite profile: in the open protocol it actually improves Core Exact to 0.506 but asks nearly twice as many questions, 9.5 versus 4.8, while in the choice protocol its removal costs 6.0 percentage points. The read is a division of labor: gap discovery contributes most directly to coverage, while guided action selection is what controls interaction length and, under the structured interface, contributes more to recovery. On the open side, the paper separates question packaging from stopping errors, and notes that the headline choice gain comes primarily from converting unresolved slots into fully restored ones, with very few partial-credit cases, which argues the improvement is concentrated on actually reaching the relevant requirement rather than on near-miss questions.

The takeaways are specific. This is a well-defined sub-problem with a clean benchmark, and the central empirical finding is that pre-formulation clarification is hard: even the strongest off-the-shelf model, Opus 4.8, does not exceed 60 percent Core Exact and still carries substantial silent assumptions. The gap-tracking-plus-action-selection decomposition is a reasonable architecture, and the ablations show each stage doing a distinct job. But the method's headline recovery advantage comes with a roughly three times interaction cost, the free-form results do not support a uniform win, and the whole evaluation rests on a simulated user and a post-hoc judge, so the numbers measure a controlled proxy rather than real stakeholder dialogue.

The benchmark construction deserves a bit more attention because it defines what the task actually is. Each instance starts from a fully specified optimization task; the pipeline withholds its formulation-critical facts, rewrites the rest into a partial public brief, and generates the interaction and evaluation artifacts: the private source-supported facts that the simulated user can disclose, the fact-bounded responses, and the slot-level recovery rubrics. Slots are prioritized, with top-priority slots being the ones whose absence most directly changes the formulation, and the Core Exact metric is defined only over the 94 of 100 cases that contain at least one of those. The choice-based protocol, MC, asks questions with three generated options, and the simulated user must pick the closest match when none is correct; the MC-D variant adds a fixed fourth option that permits a free-form correction, and that is the configuration used for the main method comparison. In the choice diagnostics, all 132 no-match events out of 1,129 audited choice events invoked that free-form correction option, which is a useful signal about how often the generated choices fail to cover the hidden fact.

The positioning against prior work is also worth stating. The closest prior systems are ORPilot, which embeds an interview stage inside a full end-to-end modeling pipeline but does not directly evaluate hidden-slot recovery or readiness, and a line of clarification work in conversational retrieval and ambiguity resolution that treats asking as a decision about when and what to ask, but targets search intents and rule conditions rather than requirements that change an optimization formulation. The paper's claim, and I think it is defensible, is that pre-formulation clarification had not been isolated as an explicit evaluation target before, and OR-Clarify is the first operations-research-specific framework to jointly evaluate gap recovery, readiness decisions, and interaction cost before autoformulation. That is a modest but real gap in the literature: prior interactive OR systems demonstrate that talking to the user helps, but they do not measure whether the agent recovered the specific facts that change the model, or whether it knows when to stop.
